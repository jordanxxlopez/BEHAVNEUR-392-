## Chapter 2, slide 06 — Actin waves accompany bursts of neurite growth

Original citation(s): Winans et al. (2016), Fig. 1b and Video 5

### Version A — Three main bullets and nested teaching bullets

- **Actin waves** are moving regions of **filamentous actin** that travel outward along immature neuronal projections called **neurites**.
  - A **neurite** can later develop into an axon or dendrite; during early development, several neurites can compete to grow.
  - Winans and colleagues tracked fluorescently labeled actin waves in cultured **rat hippocampal neurons**.
  - The waves move from the cell body toward the **growth cone** at the neurite tip.
- As an actin wave passes, the **neurite shaft briefly widens**, creating room for more **microtubules** to grow.
  - **Microtubules** are dynamic internal polymers that help support the process and provide tracks for cargo transport.
  - **Kinesin motor proteins** move cargo along microtubules, so local microtubule growth is linked to increased transport.
- **Neurite growth occurs in bursts** when actin waves arrive near the tip, rather than continuing at one constant rate.
  - The **growth cone** may extend rapidly after a wave arrives, then slow when the wave is gone.
  - Waves enter different branches at different times; this produces **uneven growth** among neurites of the same neuron.
  - During early imaging, approximately **90% of neurite outgrowth** occurred alongside arriving actin waves.

### Version B — Three connected teaching paragraphs

Immature **hippocampal neurons** extend several **neurites**, which are growing projections that can later become axons or dendrites. Within a neurite, **filamentous actin** can reorganize into a moving **actin wave**. Winans and colleagues used fluorescent labeling to track these waves in cultured rat neurons. Individual waves moved outward from the cell body toward the growing tip, while different branches received waves at different times.

As an actin wave travels through a neurite, it briefly **widens the neurite shaft**. The increased space allows additional **microtubules** to polymerize inside the process. These dynamic polymers both support the neurite and provide tracks for **kinesin motor proteins**, which carry cellular cargo toward the tip. Microtubule growth and kinesin-based transport therefore increase in regions reached by the traveling wave.

**Neurite extension occurs in bursts** associated with an actin wave reaching the **growth cone**. A branch may remain nearly the same length, then extend more rapidly as a wave arrives. Because waves travel into individual neurites at different times, the same developing neuron can have one actively growing branch while another grows more slowly. These changing patterns of transport and extension occur before the neurites acquire their mature identities.

## Chapter 2, slide 13 — Microglia engulf material from damaged or dying cells

Original citation(s): Gordon et al. (2024), Fig. 3a and time-lapse video

### Version A — Three main bullets and nested teaching bullets

- **Microglia** remove damaged-cell fragments through **phagocytosis**, a form of cellular engulfment.
  - A microglial process extends around material outside the cell, enclosing debris within a membrane-bound compartment.
  - Internalized debris is processed by the cell's degradative machinery rather than released as a neuronal signal.
  - Removing fragments changes the tissue immediately surrounding nearby neurons and other glial cells.
- **Microglia change the shape of their processes** as they interact with the surrounding neural tissue.
  - Individual extensions lengthen or retract as cells contact material in their local environment.
  - Fluorescent labeling allows investigators to follow these structural changes across a series of images.
- **Time-lapse imaging distinguishes biological time from playback time** during microglial movements.
  - In the recorded sequence, labeled microglia change between the 0-, 120-, 200-, and 210-minute observations.
  - A movie can compress several hours into seconds; the time stamps identify when the real cellular events occurred.
  - **Phagocytic clearance** proceeds on a slower timescale than the electrical signals neurons transmit.

### Version B — Three connected teaching paragraphs

**Microglia** are cells that help remove damaged or dying material from neural tissue. Through **phagocytosis**, their membrane surrounds a fragment and brings it into the cell. The internalized material can then be broken down by degradative machinery. This is a physical clearance process, distinct from a neuron releasing transmitter across a synapse. Unlike electrical communication, debris removal changes the cellular material remaining in the tissue.

A microglial cell can repeatedly **extend and retract its processes** as it contacts its surroundings. These changing projections alter where the cell touches nearby tissue, including the spaces around neurons and other glia. Labeling microglia with fluorescent markers lets researchers observe their shifting outlines and follow interactions across successive images. A process that retracts from one location can then extend toward another region of tissue.

**Time-lapse recordings** capture these changes over biological time. In the labeled sequence, the images correspond to observations at 0, 120, 200, and 210 minutes. A short playback can therefore represent several hours of movement or clearance. The time stamps describe the actual pace of microglial activity, which is much slower than the rapid electrical events of neuronal signaling. The 210-minute observation occurs three and a half hours after the initial frame.

## Chapter 2, slide 42 — Threshold initiates a regenerative action potential

Original citation(s): Carlson and Birkett (2022), Fig. 2.20, p. 52

### Version A — Three main bullets and nested teaching bullets

- An **action potential** begins when depolarization reaches the **voltage threshold** for a self-amplifying electrical response.
  - Depolarization opens **voltage-gated Na+ channels** in the neuronal membrane.
  - As Na+ enters, the inside becomes less negative and more Na+ channels open, producing **positive feedback**.
  - A small voltage change that remains below threshold does not trigger the same regenerative spike.
- The **electrochemical gradient** drives Na+ into the neuron during the rising phase of the action potential.
  - Na+ is more concentrated outside a resting neuron, and its net driving force favors entry when channels open.
  - As inward Na+ current continues, membrane voltage can cross 0 mV and briefly become positive inside.
  - Near the peak, Na+ channels become inactivated and outward K+ current contributes to repolarization.
- A normal **action potential is all-or-none** rather than a larger copy of a weak local voltage change.
  - Once the regenerative event starts, a stronger stimulus does not simply make that single spike taller.
  - Stronger input can instead change when a neuron fires and how frequently it produces spikes.
  - Na+ channel inactivation and delayed K+ movement help end the spike and return voltage toward rest.

### Version B — Three connected teaching paragraphs

An **action potential** is a rapid change in membrane voltage that regenerates once depolarization reaches **threshold**. Voltage-gated sodium channels open, allowing Na+ to enter. The resulting depolarization opens additional sodium channels, creating **positive feedback**. Below threshold, a local voltage change can remain small instead of developing into a full spike. In the plotted trace, the threshold line lies above the resting membrane potential.

Na+ enters during the rising phase because its **electrochemical driving force** favors inward movement. Sodium is more concentrated outside the resting neuron, and inward current makes the inside less negative. Continued entry can carry membrane potential above 0 mV. The illustrated voltage begins near -70 mV and rises toward +40 mV. Near the spike peak, sodium channels become inactivated, while delayed K+ efflux begins to drive the membrane voltage back toward negative values.

The full spike is **all-or-none** under comparable conditions, rather than being proportional in height to stimulus strength. A stronger stimulus can make spikes occur sooner or more frequently without simply making each action potential taller. Sodium-channel inactivation and potassium current help terminate the spike and restore voltage, preparing the membrane for subsequent electrical activity. Continued K+ movement can briefly drive membrane voltage below the resting level.

## Chapter 2, slide 52 — Presynaptic calcium triggers vesicle fusion

Original citation(s): Carlson and Birkett (2022), Fig. 2.27, p. 58

### Version A — Three main bullets and nested teaching bullets

- An arriving **action potential depolarizes the presynaptic terminal** and opens **voltage-gated Ca2+ channels**.
  - Ca2+ is much more concentrated outside the terminal than inside, so opening these channels drives Ca2+ inward.
  - The Ca2+ rise is strongest near each open channel rather than uniform throughout the entire terminal.
  - This brief local rise provides the immediate signal that activates prepared release machinery.
- **Calcium-dependent vesicle fusion** releases neurotransmitter into the **synaptic cleft**.
  - A prepared synaptic vesicle stores transmitter close to the presynaptic membrane before fusion.
  - In **exocytosis**, vesicle membrane joins the cell membrane and the vesicle contents enter the cleft.
  - Transmitter can then bind postsynaptic receptors and change the receiving cell's response.
- The **distance between calcium channels and vesicles** changes the likelihood of transmitter release.
  - A vesicle located close to an open Ca2+ channel experiences a stronger local calcium signal than a distant vesicle.
  - The number of release-ready vesicles and their positions influence how many fuse after the same spike.
  - Electrical excitation, Ca2+ entry, vesicle fusion, and receptor activation are separate physical steps.

### Version B — Three connected teaching paragraphs

When an **action potential** reaches the presynaptic terminal, depolarization opens **voltage-gated Ca2+ channels**. Calcium enters because its concentration is much greater outside the neuron and the net electrochemical force favors entry. The resulting calcium rise is highly local: a vesicle near an open channel encounters a larger transient than a vesicle farther away. The signal begins where calcium enters, beside the membrane proteins that organize vesicle release.

**Synaptic vesicles** contain neurotransmitter and can be prepared near the presynaptic membrane. Incoming Ca2+ activates the machinery that joins the vesicle membrane to the cell membrane. Through **exocytosis**, transmitter enters the synaptic cleft and can bind receptors on the postsynaptic cell. The arriving voltage signal and membrane-fusion event are therefore different steps in chemical communication. Transmitter must still cross the narrow cleft before it can activate a postsynaptic receptor.

The **amount of transmitter released** depends partly on the number of prepared vesicles and how close they lie to calcium entry sites. Two terminals receiving similar action potentials can release different amounts if their vesicles encounter different local Ca2+ signals. Spike arrival, calcium entry, vesicle fusion, and receptor activation form a causal sequence, but each step has its own cellular machinery.
