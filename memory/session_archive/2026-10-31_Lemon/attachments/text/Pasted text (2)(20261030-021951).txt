Section 1: Critical Periods Versus Sensitive Periods

A critical period is a stretch of development with a programmed start and a programmed end. Change in response to the environment, which is what we mean by learning, is strictly limited to this window. Before the window opens and after it closes, the system has no sensitivity to the environment at all.
The onset and the offset are discrete, meaning they behave like a switch being turned on and then turned off.
If the right input is missing during the window, the missing learning cannot be made up later.
A sensitive period, also called an optimal period, is a looser idea. Learning remains possible across the whole life span, but it works best during particular windows of time.
These windows can open and close gradually instead of abruptly.
The edges of a sensitive period can themselves be changed by experience, so the timing is not fully fixed by a built-in schedule.
The classic example of a critical period comes from vision. Even in that case, the term sensitive period turns out to fit better. Evidence for critical periods in language learning is then considered with this distinction in mind.

Section 2: Amblyopia as a Case of Early Deprivation

Amblyopia is poor vision in one eye even though the peripheral parts of the visual system work normally. The eye itself, including the structures that take in light, is functioning. The problem lies in how the brain has learned to use the input from that eye.
Three conditions can lead to amblyopia: strabismus, cataract, and anisometropia.
In strabismus, the two eyes are misaligned, so they do not point at the same thing.
In a cataract, the lens of the eye becomes cloudy, which blocks and blurs the light entering the eye.
In anisometropia, the two eyes have different focusing power, so one eye produces a sharper image than the other.
Each of these conditions deprives the brain of normal visual input from one eye for a period of time. The important observation is that vision can stay poor long after the condition itself has been fixed. Repairing the eye does not repair the vision, because the damage was done to how the brain organized itself while the input was abnormal.

Section 3: Competition Between Inputs in the Visual Cortex

Amblyopia occurs because of competitive dynamics between cortical inputs. The two eyes send signals to the visual cortex, and those signals compete for cortical territory. Each eye normally holds onto its own share of the cortical representation.
When vision in one eye is abnormal, the other eye can take over the cortical space that belonged to the weaker eye. This takeover is called colonization of the cortical representation by the other eye. The stronger eye wins territory simply because it is sending better signals during the period when the cortex is sorting out which input gets which space.
Once normal function returns to the weaker eye, it is still at a competitive disadvantage. Much of its cortical territory is already occupied. Normal experience, such as simply using that eye, is no longer enough to restore normal cortical responses.
A set of images of monocular deprivation in mice illustrates how quickly the cortex starts to change when one eye is deprived of normal input.
After two to three days, the small spines on neurons, which are sites where connections form, show increased motility, meaning they move around more than normal.
After three to four days, spines are lost and some axons begin to degenerate, a process called Wallerian degeneration.
After more than one week, axons sprout new branches and new spines are born from scratch, which is called de novo spine birth. The wiring of the cortex has been rebuilt around the unbalanced input.

Section 4: Treatment Based on Competition

Because amblyopia is a problem of competition, one of the most effective treatments works on competition. The treatment involves intermittently patching the “good” eye. Covering the stronger eye forces the brain to rely on the weaker eye for part of each day.
Patching restores the competitive balance between the two eyes. Once the balance is restored, the brain’s own plasticity can reorganize the cortex and bring back normal function in the weaker eye. The treatment does not repair the eye. It changes the conditions under which the cortex decides how to divide its territory.
This treatment works best early in development. By late childhood, around ten years of age, it may no longer be effective. This age pattern is what marks amblyopia as a case with a limited developmental window.

Section 5: Adult Plasticity After the Critical Period

Experiments with adults have started to show that plasticity is not abolished entirely, even well into adulthood. Some of the patients in these studies are in their fifties. The idea of a window that slams shut does not match this result.
The study by Levi and Polat (1996, PNAS) used long-term, intensive training on specific psychophysical tasks with the affected eye. Psychophysical tasks are carefully controlled perceptual tests, such as judging small differences in what is seen.
Performance improved gradually across thousands of practice trials. The data are plotted in units of kilotrials of training, where one kilotrial is one thousand trials.
The gains were not limited to the trained task. General visual function improved as well.
A further study by Li, Ngo, Nguyen, Lam, and Levi (2008, ARVO) found that playing video games enhanced visual acuity and visual attention in adults with amblyopia. Visual acuity is the sharpness of vision. A common action video game, played with the affected eye, was the kind of experience that produced the gains.

Section 6: Interim Summary on Amblyopia

Amblyopia is a classic example of a sensitive period. Three features of it carry the lesson for language.
A disturbance in the periphery during early experience can lead to life-long disruption of visual function.
The long-term loss of function is the sequela, meaning the lasting consequence, of a temporary deficit that happened during experience-dependent organization of the brain. The deficit in the input was brief, but the way the brain organized itself around it was lasting.
Treatment is most effective during a limited developmental window, although it is not necessarily useless outside that window.
New evidence also indicates that the right kind of experience can reverse deficits late into adulthood. Early deprivation therefore produces a strong disadvantage, but the door is not completely closed.

Section 7: Relating Critical Periods to Language

Two lines of work connect these ideas to language.
The first is Lenneberg’s early attempts to bring together developmental neurology and the notion of a language acquisition device. This is the idea that humans have a built-in mechanism for acquiring language.
The second is the study of early deprivation. The data come from neglected children and from Deaf children who received limited exposure to sign language.

Section 8: Lenneberg’s Biological Foundations of Language (1967)

In Biological Foundations of Language (1967), Lenneberg tried to combine what linguistics had established at the time with what was known about brain development. Both fields were producing patterns that looked like they might fit together.
Studies of language development were beginning to reveal a fairly robust, stage-like pattern. Children across different samples reached the same language milestones in the same order and at similar ages.
The curves for children in Austrian, British, and American samples describe the percentage of children reaching each milestone over the first few years of life. The milestones include producing words, producing two-word phrases, and spontaneously constructing sentences with five or more words.
A table of milestones from early infancy through the second year ties specific behaviors to specific ages. Examples include repeated syllables in babbling at about eight months, and a repertoire of several words by about eighteen months.
Language appeared to have maturational milestones. This regularity looked consistent with the unfolding of a biological program, in the same way that walking and other motor skills unfold on a schedule.

Section 9: Data on Typical Brain Development

The evidence on typical brain development in 1967 was sparse. It was almost entirely based on post-mortem analyses of tissue, meaning the examination of brains after death.
One figure plots neuron size in the cerebral cortex against age. The size of cell bodies in several cortical layers grows rapidly in the first two years of life and then levels off.
No measurements were available between the ages of two and eighteen. Brains of children who died in this age range had not been measured.
The region of the graph marked as language acquisition falls in this gap, which shows how thin the data were.
Even with these limits, Lenneberg noticed that abrupt changes in neuron size and packing density before age two might be permissive for the beginning of language acquisition. Permissive means that these changes make language learning possible without causing it directly.
The critical period for language therefore appears to open after this initial period of rapid brain development.

Section 10: Recovery from Stroke and Hemispherectomy

More extensive data came from studies of recovery from stroke. A consistent observation was that aphasia, the loss or impairment of language, is surprisingly rare in children with brain lesions. Adults with comparable damage often show severe and lasting language problems.
In extreme cases, doctors performed a hemispherectomy, which is the surgical removal of an entire hemisphere of the brain. The procedure was typically done to relieve seizures in people with epilepsy.
A table based on Basser (1962) compares outcomes by age at surgery.
Before the teenage years, removing the left hemisphere left speech unaffected or improved in most cases. In that group, 49 patients had speech not affected or improved, and 3 had permanent aphasia.
Removing the right hemisphere before the teenage years also left speech largely intact. Here, 38 patients were unaffected or improved, and 5 had permanent aphasia.
A single case of surgery during puberty showed slow improvement, with some residue remaining at the end of life, which was 27 months after the operation.
In adults, removing the left hemisphere produced no cases of unaffected or improved speech. Six adults had permanent aphasia, and one of them had aphasia before the operation. Removing the right hemisphere in adults left speech unaffected in all 25 cases.
Children who lose the left hemisphere nonetheless seem to develop typical language abilities. Adults do not recover language after this procedure. The right hemisphere in a child can take over what the left hemisphere would ordinarily do. The adult right hemisphere cannot.
This pattern, along with other evidence, led Lenneberg to hypothesize that a major factor in closing the critical period was the development of adult-like cerebral dominance. Cerebral dominance means that one hemisphere, usually the left, becomes the main hemisphere for language. Once the dominance is established, the other hemisphere can no longer substitute.

Section 11: Complications in the Stroke Evidence

Systematic studies of children with perinatal strokes make the picture more complicated. Perinatal here covers the period from prenatal life to 28 days after birth. Outcomes for these children are clearly much better than outcomes for adults with similar injuries. Two qualifications apply.
First, there are early language deficits in both right and left hemisphere patients. Language does not simply move to the healthy side without cost. Some deficits are worse for children with right hemisphere damage, which is not what a strict left-hemisphere dominance account would predict.
Second, language outcomes are generally in the low-normal range. This pattern suggests a persistent delay instead of a full catching up. The children do far better than adults, but their language does not reach the level that would be expected without the injury.

Section 12: Early Deprivation and the Case of Genie

Genie is the most well-known case of a child who was deprived of language input during childhood. Her case was described by Curtiss and colleagues (1974). She was born in April 1957 and was discovered when she was 13 years and 7 months old.
When she was found, Genie was severely malnourished. She was painfully thin and looked about six or seven years old. She could not stand up straight or chew food, and she was not toilet trained. She did not speak, did not cry, and did not produce any vocal sounds.
From the age of 20 months, Genie had been confined to a small room under conditions of extreme restraint.
Most of the time she was strapped into an infant’s potty chair. At night she was confined in a sleeping bag inside a crib covered with wire mesh.
She received minimal care from her mother, who was herself rapidly losing her sight. She was fed only infant food.
Her father was physically punishing her whenever she made any sounds.
Very little is known about exactly how much language Genie heard during her isolation. What is known points to almost none.
Her father could not tolerate noise, and there was no television or radio in the home.
Her father and older brother did not speak to her. At times they barked at her like dogs instead.
For the most part she was alone for hours at a time, day after day, year after year.
Genie’s case matters for the critical period question because her language input was cut off long before puberty. She was then exposed to language for the first time after the age at which Lenneberg proposed the window closes.

Section 13: What Genie Learned

Genie’s language was observed for four years after she was found. During that time she learned quite a bit, which rules out the strongest version of a critical period. A strict critical period would predict no language learning at all after the window closed.
She came to comprehend hundreds of words. Vocabulary was one of her strongest areas.
She came to comprehend some aspects of morphology. Morphology is the part of grammar that deals with the pieces that make up words, such as endings that mark plurals or past tense. Genie was particularly good with plurals, meaning she understood that adding an ending can change one object into many.
She also came to comprehend some prepositions, which are words like on, in, under, and behind that express where things are relative to each other.
In one test, Genie was given buttons and plastic boxes of different colors and sizes. She was asked to move them according to instructions, such as “Put the red box behind the blue box.”
She was mostly correct with prepositions like on and in. On in, for example, she was correct 37 times and incorrect only 4 times.
Her performance was less consistent on some others, but she was correct more often than not on every preposition tested.

Section 14: What Genie Did Not Learn

Genie’s production lagged severely behind her comprehension. She understood far more than she could say.
Some lag between comprehension and production is typical of normal development. Young children also understand words before they can produce them.
Her history adds another factor. She had been punished for making sounds as a child, which may have affected her willingness or ability to speak.
Her grammatical abilities developed much more slowly than her vocabulary. She could keep adding words, but building and understanding the structure of sentences was far harder for her.
A test of conjunctions captures this grammatical difficulty. Five familiar objects were placed in a row in front of her. She was asked to point to one or more of them in response to sentences.
Some sentences used and, such as “Show me the fork and pencil.” Genie was correct on almost all of these, with 100 correct and 3 incorrect.
Other sentences used or, such as “Show me the crayon or the knife.” She was correct on only 7 of these and incorrect on 51.
Early on, she treated or sentences the same way as and sentences, pointing to both objects. Later, she began to respond differently to and and or, but she still did not understand the meaning of either/or.
The word or changes the logical meaning of a sentence instead of simply adding one more item. This kind of grammatical meaning was much harder for her than learning the names of objects.

Section 15: Open Questions About Genie

Genie’s case leaves many questions unanswered, which makes it hard to draw firm conclusions.
One question is whether Genie had a learning disability that was present from birth. If she did, her difficulties with grammar might not be caused by the timing of her language exposure at all.
Another question is how her social development was affected by the lasting impact of neglect and abuse. Language is learned in social interaction, so damage to her social development could also limit her language.
After four years, funding to study Genie was withdrawn. As a result, we cannot know what she might have learned if she had been observed for longer.
Even with these limits, the disparity between her vocabulary development and her grammatical development is widely interpreted as evidence about sensitive periods. The interpretation is that grammar learning in a first language is specifically impaired when exposure begins after a sensitive period. Words can still be learned late, but grammar seems to depend more on early exposure.

Section 16: Deaf Children with Delayed Sign Language Input

Many deaf children do not receive sign language input early in life. Unlike Genie, these children have an otherwise typical upbringing. They are cared for, fed, and raised in ordinary social settings. What they lack is access to a language they can perceive, since they cannot hear the spoken language around them.
This situation provides a cleaner test of the sensitive period idea. The children are deprived of language input, but not of care, nutrition, or social contact.
The study by Mayberry, Lock, and Kazmi (2002, Nature) compared deaf individuals with different early language experiences.
Children aged 9 to 15 who were learning American Sign Language (ASL) as their first language had difficulty learning it.
A comparison group consisted of children who became deaf after first learning a spoken language. These children did much better at learning ASL, even though they were starting ASL at a similar age.
These differences persist into adulthood.
The first part of the study measured ASL performance in deaf adults using a task that required recalling complex ASL sentences.
Deaf adults who had experienced no language in early life performed poorly, at under 40 percent.
Deaf adults who had experienced a spoken language in early life performed much better, at close to 80 percent.
Both groups learned ASL at a later age. The difference is whether they had any first language early in childhood.
The second part of the study measured English performance using a task where participants judged whether complex written English sentences were grammatically correct. Chance performance on this task is 50 percent.
Deaf adults with no early language performed only slightly above chance, at around 52 percent.
Deaf adults who had learned ASL in infancy performed at around 80 percent.
Hearing adults who had learned a spoken language other than English in infancy also performed at around 80 percent.
The pattern across both parts of the study points to the importance of having any first language early in life. It does not matter much whether that first language is signed or spoken. Early exposure to a language supports later learning of another language, including a language in a different form. Lacking early language exposure harms learning of the first language and also harms learning of later languages.

Section 17: Critical Period or Sensitive Period for a First Language?

Recovery from stroke looks very different in childhood than in adulthood. Recovery from hemispherectomy looks even more different. Children who lose an entire hemisphere can still develop language, while adults who lose the left hemisphere do not recover it.
Deprivation of input does not appear to extend the critical period. In some systems, one might expect that a window stays open longer if the needed input never arrives. Genie and the deaf children who received sign language late suggest that this does not happen for language. The window seems to close on schedule whether or not language input was present.
All of these data come from highly unusual cases with many other factors involved. Brain injury, epilepsy, neglect, and abuse all bring additional problems that are hard to separate from the timing of language exposure. These complications make it difficult to reach firm conclusions.
There is also a lack of animal models for human language. In vision research, scientists can deprive animals of input or manipulate genes to test critical periods directly. Gene knockout experiments, where a gene is deliberately switched off to see its effect, cannot be done in people. Research on language critical periods must therefore rely on natural cases that are rare and complicated.
