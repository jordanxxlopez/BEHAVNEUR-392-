Section 1: The Problem Speech Perception Has to Solve

Speech reaches us as a messy physical signal, yet we pull a clear message out of it. When someone talks, their tongue, lips, jaw, and throat are constantly moving. A real-time MRI image from the USC SPAN group captures this. It is a side view of a talker’s head, with colored lines tracing the edges of the tongue, the lips, the roof of the mouth, and the back of the throat. These shapes keep changing as the person speaks, and each change alters the sound that comes out. Somehow listeners take this constantly shifting stream and recover words and meaning from it.
A spectrogram turns that sound stream into a picture of frequency over time. The spectrogram of the sentence “I can see you” runs from 0 to about 1.9 seconds on the horizontal axis. The vertical axis shows frequency from 0 to 7,000 cycles per second, which is the same as hertz (Hz). Darker regions mean more sound energy at that frequency and moment.
The vowels show up as dark horizontal bands that bend up and down as the mouth changes shape. In “I” and “you,” these bands curve smoothly from one position to another.
Around 0.4 to 0.55 seconds there is a nearly blank stretch, which is the brief silence while the mouth closes for the “c” in “can.” A thin vertical spike follows when that closure is released.
The “s” in “see,” at roughly 1.0 to 1.2 seconds, looks completely different from the vowels. Its energy is a dark smear concentrated high up, from about 4,000 to 7,000 Hz, with no clear bands.
There are no clean gaps between the words. The signal flows continuously, and the word boundaries we hear are not marked in the sound itself.
We also get the same message from very different physical signals. People of different ages, sexes, and body sizes all say the same words, but their voices are physically different. A child, a grandmother, and an adult man can all say “see,” and we hear the same word every time.
Vowel measurements from many speakers overlap heavily. A classic chart plots the second formant frequency against the first formant frequency for ten English vowels, produced by 76 speakers. Formants will be defined in detail shortly. For now, they are the main frequency bands that define a vowel. Each vowel’s measurements form a cloud of points, circled by an oval. The ovals are large, and many of them overlap. That means the same pair of measurements could come from one vowel for one speaker and a different vowel for another speaker. Listeners still identify the vowels correctly, which is part of what makes speech perception so impressive.
Section 2: From a Physical Signal to Symbols

One premise is that the brain must convert a variable, analog signal into something more abstract. An analog signal varies continuously, with no natural steps or breaks. The sound of speech is like this. It changes smoothly in frequency and loudness, and it changes from speaker to speaker and from moment to moment. The language system, however, works with discrete units, like individual sounds, words, and grammatical pieces. So the brain needs some way to translate the continuous sound into these separate, abstract units before the language system can use it.
Categorical perception is thought to reflect this translation from the physical to the symbolic. Categorical perception means hearing a continuous range of sounds as belonging to a small number of distinct categories. Instead of hearing every tiny acoustic difference, listeners sort sounds into groups, such as /b/ or /d/. The idea is that this sorting is the step where the physical signal becomes a symbol the language system can operate on.
This view is one premise, not the only one. Other researchers have proposed alternative ways of thinking about how listeners get from sound to meaning. Those alternatives come up in the next session.

Section 5: Measuring a Vowel’s Formants

Formants can be explored directly through source-filter synthesis. In the source-filter view of speech, the vocal folds produce a raw sound called the source. The vocal tract then acts as a filter that strengthens some frequencies and weakens others. Synthesis means building a sound artificially. By separating a recorded vowel into its source and its filter, then changing the filter, we can create new vowels and hear exactly what each formant does. Section 7: Extracting the Source

The source can be pulled out of a recording by removing the filter. Praat used a model of the filter, built from the measured formant frequencies, to estimate what the vocal tract was doing to the sound. It then removed that filtering effect from the recording. What remains is an estimate of the raw sound produced by the vocal folds.
The process resembles multiple regression. In multiple regression, you explain a measured outcome using several known predictors, and whatever the predictors cannot explain is left over. Here, the formant-based filter model accounts for part of the sound. The leftover part is treated as the source.
The extracted source is a mostly unfiltered version of the voice, and it sounds like a buzz. In the spectrogram of the source, the energy is spread fairly evenly across all frequencies up to 5,000 Hz. There are no clear dark bands, because the formants have been removed. The waveform is also much smaller in amplitude, with values around 0.02 instead of nearly 0.9 in the original. Without the vocal tract’s shaping, the sound no longer has a vowel quality. It is just the steady buzz of the vocal folds vibrating.

Section 10: Formants and Vowel Space

Formant values depend partly on the speaker’s anatomy. People differ in the size and shape of their vocal tracts, even when they speak the same language. A longer vocal tract tends to resonate at different frequencies than a shorter one. So two speakers can say the same vowel and still produce different formant values.
Formant values also depend on speaking rate and context. When people talk quickly, they may not reach the full tongue position for each vowel. Formants also depend on the surrounding sounds. The phones produced before and after a vowel pull its formants in different directions. A phone is a single speech sound as it is actually produced.

Section 11: The Pattern Playback Synthesizer

The pattern playback synthesizer turned painted spectrograms into sound. It was an early machine for creating speech-like sounds. Researchers painted simple patterns onto a clear sheet, using white strokes to mark where they wanted energy at each frequency and moment. The machine then played that painted pattern back as sound. This let researchers build speech sounds with precise control over each formant.
The machine worked through a chain of optical and electrical steps.
A light source sent out a beam, which passed through a cylindrical lens that shaped the light into a line.
The light then passed through a spinning tone wheel. The wheel broke the light into many separate frequency components.
A second lens focused this light, and a mirror set at 45 degrees directed it onto the painted spectrogram.
The spectrogram sat on a moving belt, so the pattern passed under the light from left to right, just as time runs left to right in a spectrogram.
Wherever paint was present, light was reflected into a light collector and photocell. A second light collector picked up light transmitted through the sheet. The photocell converted the light into an electrical signal.
That signal went to an amplifier and then to a loudspeaker, which produced the sound.
The result was that the painted shapes determined which frequencies were heard and when. A stroke painted at a certain height produced energy at that frequency. A stroke that slanted upward produced a frequency that rose over time. One of the photographs is a close-up of such a painted pattern, with short white bars and slanted strokes laid out on a grid.
Section 12: Formant Transitions in Stop Consonants

Stop consonants are distinguished by formant transitions. Stop consonants are sounds like /b/, /d/, and /g/, made by completely blocking the airflow and then releasing it. A formant transition is a change in formant frequency over time, usually at the start or end of a vowel. In a spectrogram, a transition looks like the beginning of a formant band bending up or down before it levels off.
This makes sense given how stops are produced. The audible part of /b/, /d/, or /g/ happens while the jaw is opening. As the mouth moves from the closed position into the vowel, the shape of the vocal tract changes quickly. That rapid change in shape makes the formants slide in frequency. Each stop consonant blocks the air at a different place in the mouth, so each one produces a different pattern of formant movement as the mouth opens.
The pattern playback synthesizer was used to create speech-like sounds that copied these transitions. Liberman et al. (1957) painted a set of 14 two-formant patterns. Each pattern lasted about 300 milliseconds. The frequency axis runs from 0 to 2,400 cycles per second.
In every pattern, the first formant starts low and rises quickly into a steady level near 300 Hz.
The second formant settles at a steady level near 2,000 to 2,200 Hz in every pattern. The only thing that differs is where it starts.
In stimuli 1 through 6, the second formant starts below its steady level and rises into it. Stimulus 1 has the lowest starting point and the steepest rise.
In stimulus 7, the second formant is nearly flat.
In stimuli 8 through 14, the second formant starts above its steady level and falls into it. The starting point gets higher with each stimulus, so the fall gets steeper.
Section 13: Building a Speech Continuum

The Liberman stimuli form a speech continuum. A continuum is a series of stimuli that change gradually in one physical property, in equal steps, from one end to the other. In this case, the first formant was kept constant across all stimuli. Only the onset frequency of the second formant changed. The F2 onset frequency is the frequency where the second formant begins its transition.
The F2 onset frequency was varied linearly from stimulus 1 to 13. Changing something linearly means changing it by the same fixed amount at every step. So moving from stimulus 1 to stimulus 2 changes the F2 onset by the same number of hertz as moving from stimulus 8 to stimulus 9.
Adjacent stimuli differ by the same amount across the entire continuum. This design gives the experiment a clear acoustic baseline. Physically, every neighboring pair is equally different. Any difference in how listeners perceive those pairs must therefore come from the listener, not from the sounds themselves.
Section 14: An Ideal Observer Model Without Categories: Labeling

An ideal observer model makes predictions about what perception would look like under a stated assumption. An ideal observer is a theoretical listener who uses the information in the stimulus in a specific, defined way. Here the assumption is that perception is non-categorical. That means listeners would hear the F2 onset as a smooth, continuous property, with no special sorting into groups.
Under this model, labeling would get blurry as stimuli move away from the category prototypes. A prototype is the clearest, most typical example of a category. On this continuum, the two endpoints are /b/ and /g/, and /d/ lies in the middle. Between these points, a stimulus becomes more ambiguous the farther it sits from a prototype. A stimulus that is closer to /b/ than to /d/ would be labeled /b/ most of the time, but not always. Section 15: An Ideal Observer Model Without Categories: Discrimination

The second prediction of the linear model is that discrimination should be independent of labeling. Discrimination is the ability to tell whether two sounds are the same or different. Under the linear model, how well a listener can tell two stimuli apart has nothing to do with what names the listener would give them.
The labeling curves have peaks for a simple reason. There are three categories, and each label is a yes-or-no decision for every stimulus. A listener either calls a sound /d/ or does not. With three categories spread along one continuum, the middle category has to rise and then fall, which gives it a peaked shape. That shape comes from the structure of the labeling task, not from anything special about perception.
The discrimination task does not depend on category names at all. In discrimination, listeners hear two stimuli and simply say “same” or “different.” If listeners heard differences in F2 onset equally well everywhere on the continuum, then accuracy would be the same for every pair.
The predicted discrimination curve is flat. The model’s graph tests pairs of stimuli two steps apart: 1 vs. 3, 2 vs. 4, 3 vs. 5, and so on up to 9 vs. 11. Accuracy is predicted to sit at the same level, about 0.8, for every pair. Each pair differs by the same physical amount, so each pair should be equally easy to tell apart. Any result that departs from this flat line would suggest that perception is not simply linear.
