2. SCIENCE FIRST — DIRECT TEACHING, NOT ABOUT-THE-SCIENCE COMMENTARY
- Make every sentence teach a real concept, mechanism, structure, experiment, observation, result, comparison, or scientifically meaningful consequence.
- Explain WHAT a process is, HOW its parts work, WHAT causes the next event, and WHAT changes in the cell, circuit, organism, or behavior. Do this naturally, not by labeling every point as 'what/how/why'.
- Define unfamiliar terms when first used and connect them to the actual phenomenon. Advanced high-school accessible language; scientifically rigorous and appropriately precise.
- Explain physical and biological steps in complete sentences. Do NOT replace a mechanism with 'this contributes to', 'this highlights', 'this helps us understand', 'this demonstrates the importance of', 'this creates a foundation for', or a vague generalized conclusion. If a sentence contributes no new scientific content, remove or replace it with a relevant explanation of the source material.
- When teaching an experiment, explain the studied system, what was manipulated or measured, what actually happened, and what the result means. Preserve meaningful distinctions between an observation, a correlation, a hypothesis, and an established causal result. State uncertainty only when scientifically important, not as empty hedging.
- Do not default to 'the researchers proposed' or finish with general statements about future development when a concrete mechanism or evidence from the same source can be taught instead. Attribution is useful for a specific study, not as a substitute for the science.
- Explain key figures' scientific information when it adds essential results or mechanism. Do NOT narrate what the viewer is looking at ('the left image shows', 'as you can see', 'in this figure') or describe decorative visuals. Keep all existing visual assets unchanged.
- Preserve the topic order and all distinct educational points. Do not add unrelated concepts, novel examples, or tangential studies to create the appearance of depth.
- Keep important numbers, conditions, equations, and labels when they are needed for the science. Avoid listing every measurement or curve value when the relationship is what matters.

3. PROFESSOR-STYLE SPEAKING VOICE — APPLY LATEST PROMPTS 1–3
- Write direct, self-contained explanations that can be read naturally aloud to students. Use complete, connected sentences, not telegraphic definitions or reference-sheet fragments.
- Keep technical terms accurate, but explain them in plain, smooth language. Mix shorter and moderately long sentences; break up tongue-twisting phrases, heavy noun clusters, and repeated or awkward word patterns.
- Do NOT say 'this slide', 'this lecture', 'the professor said', 'we covered', 'now let's look', 'moving on', 'remember', 'the key takeaway', 'this illustration shows', or anything about the presentation, writing, reader's attention, or sequence of teaching.
- Avoid rhetorical questions, fake enthusiasm, poetry, unsupported analogies, fluffy transitions, summary-only closing sentences, and artificially repeated sentences.
- Do not speak around the research. Teach its methods, mechanisms, observed findings, and what those findings specifically mean.
- Bold genuinely central terms, causal links, named phenomena, and experiments where appropriate inside the revised text, without indiscriminate emphasis.
- No arrow chains or dense symbolic shortcuts for multi-step biological processes. Use full scientific sentences.
- Equations and nomenclature must remain scientifically accurate, without inventing quantitative content.

4. THE TWO ALTERNATIVE TEXT FORMATS — TEST ON ONE REAL SLIDE FIRST
Use the SAME existing slide and scientific content when preparing the comparison. Do not change any non-text assets in either example.

VERSION A — THREE TEACHING GROUPS WITH BULLETS AND SUB-BULLETS
- Replace the original three paragraphs with THREE corresponding main teaching bullets, in the original conceptual order. Each main bullet introduces and directly teaches one substantial concept in two to five natural explanatory sentences, where space allows.
- Use nested sub-bullets where they genuinely clarify parts of a mechanism, an experimental procedure, an observation, or a causal connection. Each sub-bullet should contain one to three complete explanatory sentences, not labels or fragments.
- The three groups should build a coherent spoken explanation; do not mechanically split sentences just to fill bullets. Do not repeat the main bullet in its sub-bullet.
- For experiments, a useful natural progression is experimental system/measurement, observed result, and biological interpretation. For mechanisms, use a cause-and-effect explanation, not shorthand.
- Bullets are a visual structure ONLY: the writing must sound like a continuous spoken teaching script.

VERSION B — THREE CONNECTED, NATURAL TEACHING PARAGRAPHS
- Keep precisely THREE body-text paragraphs, each a real, developed explanation. Keep the conceptual order and preserve the same scientific meaning as Version A.
- Paragraph 1 usually establishes and explains the scientific phenomenon; paragraph 2 teaches the mechanism or experimental evidence; paragraph 3 develops the concrete consequence, observed result, or continuing mechanism. Adapt to the actual material; do not force generic roles.
- Each paragraph must contain actual instruction, not scene-setting, restatement, or a concluding slogan. Explain what physically or behaviorally happens using connected, readable sentences.
- Avoid vague closing paragraphs about what 'matters' without teaching any additional fact.

5. LENGTH AND FIT

- Do not reduce font size, expand text boxes, move visuals, change slide size, or hide overflow to force text to fit. Prefer concise but complete causal explanations over repeated general statements. If the original available space genuinely cannot hold the scientific content, flag the slide instead of silently altering other elements.
- Preserve all important original scientific content; improve the writing rather than making a shallow summary.

- Do not use AI-generated or AI-altered scientific figures, animal photos, microscopy, anatomical illustrations, or purported documentary photographs.
