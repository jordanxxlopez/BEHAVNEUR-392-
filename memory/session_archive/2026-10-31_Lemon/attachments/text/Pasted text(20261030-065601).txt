Section 1: The Messiness of Continuous Speech

Continuous speech has no physical boundaries between words. In writing, we put a space between every word, so we tend to assume speech has gaps too. It does not. In a spoken sentence such as “I hope it’s in my desk,” the sound energy runs from one word into the next with no consistent silence where one word ends and the next begins.
A spectrogram displays sound over time, with darker regions marking where the sound has more energy at a given frequency. In a spectrogram of that sentence, the dark bands of energy flow straight across word edges. Nothing in the signal marks “hope” off from “it’s,” or “my” off from “desk.”
The pitch track above the spectrogram records how the voice rises and falls. The pitch goes up and down across the sentence, but those rises and falls do not line up neatly with the edges of words either.
Pauses in speech do not reliably mark word boundaries. There are short silent stretches in the signal, but they come from closures. A closure happens when the lips or tongue briefly block the airflow to make a consonant like /p/, /t/, or /d/. While the mouth is closed, almost no sound comes out, so the spectrogram goes blank for a moment.
These closures happen wherever such a consonant falls. That can be in the middle of a word just as easily as at the edge of one. So a silent gap tells the listener that a consonant like /p/ or /t/ was produced. It does not tell the listener that a word just ended.
Listeners are still very good at finding words in this “sea of sounds.” Adults hear a sentence as a string of separate words, even though the signal itself is continuous. This raises two questions. The first is how we manage to find word boundaries at all. The second, harder question is how infants learn to do this in the first place, when they do not yet know which sound sequences are words.
Section 2: Cues to Word Boundaries: Isolated Words and Phonotactics

Some words appear in isolation. Words such as “Hello,” “No,” “Please,” “Go,” and “Stop” are often spoken alone. When a word is spoken by itself, its boundaries are obvious, since silence comes before and after it. An infant who hears “No!” on its own many times gets a clean example of where that word starts and stops.
This cue is limited, though. Most words are almost never spoken alone. Words like “the,” “of,” or “kingdom” nearly always show up inside longer stretches of speech. So isolated words can help with a small set of words, but they cannot explain how children find most of the words they learn.
Phonotactics is a second cue. Phonotactics refers to the rules and patterns for which sequences of phonemes, the individual speech sounds of a language, can appear together. Some sequences of sounds are very unlikely to occur inside a single English word.
In “wordboundary,” the /d/ at the end of “word” sits right next to the /b/ at the start of “boundary.” English words rarely contain a /d/ followed directly by a /b/. When a listener hears that pair, it is a good sign that a word boundary falls between them.
In “speechcode,” the sound at the end of “speech” is followed by the /k/ sound at the start of “code.” This pairing is also rare inside English words, so it again points to a boundary.
“Movement” is an important exception. It contains a /v/ followed by an /m/, which is the kind of unusual pairing that often signals a boundary. Yet “movement” is a single word. So phonotactic cues are helpful, but they are not perfect. A rare sound pair makes a boundary likely, not certain.
Section 3: Stress Patterns as a Cue (Jusczyk et al., 1999)

Most English words are stressed on the first syllable. Stress is the extra emphasis a syllable receives, which usually makes it louder, longer, and higher in pitch. Words like “KINGdom,” “BAby,” and “PRETty” all carry their stress on the first syllable. Since this pattern is so common, a stressed syllable in English connected speech is often the start of a new word.
Jusczyk et al. (1999) tested whether infants use this stress pattern to find words in connected speech.
Setup: Infants who were 7.5 months old were first familiarized with a single word. Familiarizing means letting them hear the word repeatedly so it becomes known to them. One group heard “kingdom,” which is stressed on its first syllable. Another group heard “device,” which is stressed on its second syllable.
Test passages: The infants then heard short passages of connected speech. In the kingdom passage, the word “kingdom” appears several times inside full sentences, such as “Your kingdom is in a faraway place” and “One day he saw a ghost in this old kingdom.” In the device passage, the word “device” appears several times in the same way, such as “Your device can do a lot” and “The pink device sews clothes.”
Observation: Infants familiarized with “kingdom” showed a preference for the kingdom passage. This means they listened longer to the passage that contained the word they already knew. Infants familiarized with “device” did not show the same kind of preference for the device passage.
Interpretation: To prefer the kingdom passage, infants had to pull the word “kingdom” out of the flowing sentences and recognize it as the word they had heard before. They could do this for a word that begins with a stressed syllable. For “device,” the stressed syllable comes second. If infants treat a stressed syllable as the start of a word, then the stress in “device” points to the wrong place. That would make the word harder to pick out of connected speech.
Conclusion: In connected speech, stress may help signal the beginnings of words. Infants as young as 7.5 months appear to use this cue.
The stress cue has clear limits. It will not work in every language, since languages differ in where they usually place stress. It also will not work for every English word, since words like “device” are stressed on a later syllable. Like phonotactics, stress is a useful hint that is right much of the time but not always.
Section 4: Transitional Probabilities as a Cue to Word Boundaries

Transitional probability is another cue to word boundaries. A transitional probability is the likelihood that one particular syllable will come next, given the syllable just heard. It measures how well one syllable predicts the following one.
The phrase “pretty baby” makes this concrete. It contains four syllables: “pre,” “ty,” “ba,” and “by.” Two of these syllable pairs fall inside a word, and one pair crosses a word boundary.
Inside the word “pretty,” the syllable /prɪ/ is followed by /ti/. Across English words, when we hear /prɪ/, the syllable /ti/ is very likely to come next. So /prɪ/ strongly predicts /ti/.
Across the word boundary, “ty” is followed by “ba.” The syllable “ty” ends many different words, and those words can be followed by almost any other word. So after “ty,” the syllable “ba” is only one of a huge number of possible next syllables. “Ty” does a poor job of predicting “ba.”

Section 5: Transitional Probabilities Stated More Generally

The general principle is that the likelihood of syllable B following syllable A is greater within words than between words. Inside a word, the syllables always come in the same order, so each one predicts the next quite well. Between words, the next syllable depends on which word happens to come next, and that could be almost anything.
A learner who tracks these probabilities can use them to find boundaries. Where the probability between two syllables is high, the syllables probably belong to the same word. Where the probability suddenly drops, a word boundary is likely.
This cue is probabilistic, not absolute. It points to the likely answer, but it can sometimes lead a listener to the wrong split. Two examples make this clear.
“What’s that in the road ahead?” can be heard as ending with “road ahead” or as “road a head.” The sounds are the same either way. The statistics alone do not settle which split is correct.
“I scream, you scream, we all scream for ice cream” plays on the fact that “I scream” and “ice cream” sound nearly identical. The same string of sounds can be divided in two different places. Transitional probabilities make some divisions more likely than others, but they cannot remove every ambiguity.
Section 6: Isolating Statistical Information from Other Cues (Saffran et al., 1996)

Saffran et al. (1996) set out to show that infants can use transitional probabilities alone to identify words. To do this, the researchers had to remove every other cue to word boundaries. If any other cue remained, it would be impossible to tell whether infants were using the statistics or that other cue.
The stimuli were tightly controlled in three ways.
The speech was monotone. There were no changes in pitch, duration, or loudness, which is also called amplitude. This removed stress as a cue, since stress depends on exactly those changes.
The syllables were all simple CV syllables. CV stands for consonant followed by vowel, as in “to,” “ki,” or “bu.” Since every syllable had the same simple shape, there were no rare sound pairings at word edges. This removed phonotactic cues to word boundaries.
The syllables were presented continuously, with no pauses. This removed silence as a possible cue.
The “words” were invented three-syllable sequences, such as tokibu, gikoba, gopila, and tipolu. These words were strung together in varying order to form one long, unbroken stream of syllables.
When written out, the stream looks like “tokibugikobagopilatipolutokibugopila…” with no breaks. An infant listening to it hears exactly this kind of uninterrupted sequence.
When each word is marked off, the hidden structure becomes clear. The stream is made entirely of the same four words, repeated in different orders.
The only information that marks the word boundaries in this stream is transitional probability. Inside a word such as “tokibu,” the syllable “to” is always followed by “ki,” and “ki” is always followed by “bu.” So the probability of each next syllable inside a word is very high. At the end of a word, however, “bu” can be followed by the first syllable of any of the other words. So the probability of the next syllable drops at the word edge. An infant who tracks these probabilities could find the words. An infant who cannot track them would have no way to do so.
Section 7: Testing for an Effect of Transitional Probabilities

A simple familiarity test would not be enough. After infants listen to a long stream of syllables, it is easy to show that they are more familiar with sequences they have heard than with sequences they have never heard. That result would only prove that infants remember sounds. It would not prove they used transitional probabilities to find words.
Two additional design details make the test meaningful.
Half of the words were repeated more frequently than the other half. Some words appeared in the stream more often than others.
The test compared real words against “part words” that were matched for frequency of occurrence. A part word is a three-syllable sequence that crosses a word boundary. It takes the end of one word and the beginning of the next word. Since the frequent words appeared so often next to each other, some part words occurred just as often in the stream as the less frequent real words did.
Together, these details mean the real words and the part words had been heard equally often. So if infants treated them differently, the difference could not come from how often they had heard each one. It could only come from the transitional probabilities, which are high inside real words and lower inside part words.
A short stream makes the logic concrete: “tokibu gikoba gopila gikoba tokibu gopila tokibu gikoba gikoba tokibu.”

Section 8: A Novelty Effect

Infants looked longer toward the part-word stimuli than toward the real words. In this kind of test, each sound plays from a particular side, and the time infants spend looking toward that side is a measure of their interest. Looking time for part words was higher than for real words, and this difference was statistically significant.
This result is a novelty effect. A novelty effect happens when infants pay more attention to something that seems new or unfamiliar to them. Infants treated the part words as less familiar than the real words, even though both had occurred equally often in the stream.
Interpretation: The infants had learned the statistical regularities of the speech stream. They were not merely picking up on how often each sequence occurred. If frequency were all that mattered, the words and part words would have seemed equally familiar, and looking times would have been the same.
Conclusion: Infants can track how well one syllable predicts the next and use that information to group syllables into word-like units. They can do this even when stress, phonotactics, and pauses have all been removed from the signal.
Section 9: Identifying Word Referents

Finding words in the speech stream is only the first step. Once children can pull a word like “cat” out of connected speech, they still have to learn what it means. This requires identifying the word’s referent, which is the thing in the world that the word refers to.
The inductive problem of early word learning describes how hard this is. Suppose a child hears “cat” while looking at a gray cat walking by. The word could refer to the whole animal. But it could just as well refer to many other things present in the same moment.
It could name a property of the animal, such as furry or alive.
It could name a broader category, such as animal or pet.
It could name a part of the animal, such as its leg or its whiskers.
It could name the action, such as walking.
It could even be the animal’s own name, such as Felix.
Every one of these meanings fits what the child sees and hears. Nothing in the single moment of hearing the word tells the child which meaning is correct. This is what makes it an inductive problem. The child has to reach a general conclusion about the word’s meaning from limited examples that are consistent with many different conclusions.
This problem is related to Quine’s “gavagai” problem. The philosopher Quine imagined a person hearing a speaker of an unknown language say “gavagai” as a rabbit runs past. The listener might guess that “gavagai” means “rabbit,” but the word could just as well refer to a part of the rabbit or to some other aspect of the scene. Quine used this example to argue that the reference of a word cannot be fully determined from the situation alone. Young children face the same problem with every new word they hear.
Section 10: Joint Attention

Joint attention is one way children narrow down what a word means. Joint attention means sharing an attentional focus with another person. Two people look at each other and at the same object, and each is aware that the other is attending to that object.
When joint attention happens, three things are linked at once. Person A is attending to the object. Person B is attending to the same object. And the two people are attending to each other, so each knows the other is focused on that object.
Gaze following emerges around 9 months of age. Gaze following means that when an adult turns to look at something, the infant also turns to look at it. This allows the infant to share the adult’s focus.
Joint attention signals an understanding of reference. When an infant follows an adult’s gaze to an object, the infant is treating the adult’s attention as being about something in the world. This is the basic idea behind reference: a word, a look, or a point can be directed at a specific thing. An infant who understands this can use the speaker’s focus to figure out what a new word is about.
Section 11: Joint Attention Optimizes Word Learning (Baldwin et al.)

Joint attention optimizes word learning. Children learn new words best when the person naming an object is clearly attending to that object together with them.
Baldwin et al. tested this by comparing two ways of hearing a new word.
Setup: In both conditions, a baby was playing with an object, and a novel word was used to label that same object. The difference was who provided the label and whether the baby could see them.
In the joint attention group, an adult who was interacting with the baby labeled the object the baby was playing with. The baby could see the adult and could tell that the adult was focused on the same object.
In the no joint attention group, a voice from behind a screen labeled the object the baby was playing with. The baby heard the word while holding the object, but could not see the speaker or tell what the speaker was attending to.
Observation: 18-month-olds learned the novel word in the joint attention group but not in the no joint attention group.
Interpretation: In both groups, the word was heard at the same moment the baby was focused on the object. So simply pairing a word with an object in time was not enough. The babies learned the word only when they could see that the speaker was attending to the object as well.
Conclusion: By 18 months, children do not just link any word they hear to whatever they happen to be looking at. They rely on shared attention with the speaker to decide what a new word refers to.

Section 12: Intentional Cues from the Speaker: Eye Gaze Direction

Children narrow the hypothesis space for new words partly by using intentional cues from the speaker. The hypothesis space is the full set of possible meanings a new word could have. For “cat,” that space includes the whole animal, its parts, its properties, its action, and its name. Learning a word means cutting this large set down to the correct meaning.
Intentional cues are signals that reveal what the speaker means to talk about. They tell the child what is on the speaker’s mind, rather than what is simply nearby or what the child happens to be holding. One of the most important intentional cues is eye gaze direction. When a speaker looks at a particular object while saying a new word, the look is strong evidence that the word refers to that object.
Baldwin studied how infants use a speaker’s gaze when learning new words.
Participants: Two age groups were taught new words. One group was 16 to 17 months old, and the other was 18 to 19 months old.
Follow-in labeling: In this condition, the speaker looked at the toy that the infant was already focused on while saying the new word. The infant’s attention and the speaker’s attention were on the same toy. So the toy the infant was looking at and the toy the speaker meant were one and the same.
Discrepant labeling: In this condition, the speaker looked at a different toy, one that was in a bucket, while saying the new word. The infant was focused on one toy, but the speaker was looking at another.
Logic of the comparison: In discrepant labeling, the infant hears the word while looking at one toy, yet the speaker’s gaze points to a different toy. This sets two possible strategies against each other. An infant could simply link the word to whatever it is looking at. Or the infant could check where the speaker is looking and link the word to that toy instead. Comparing the two conditions reveals whether infants use the speaker’s eye gaze to work out the referent, and whether this ability differs between the younger and older age groups.
Section 13: Intentional Cues from the Speaker: Affective and Behavioral Cues

Speakers also reveal their intentions through affective and behavioral cues. Affective cues are signals of emotion, such as a facial expression of excitement, surprise, or disappointment. Behavioral cues are signals from what the speaker does, such as reaching for something or acting on it. Both can show which object or event the speaker has in mind.
Akhtar et al. found that infants used adults’ facial expressions to identify the intended referent of a novel word.
When an adult reacts emotionally while using a new word, that reaction tells the infant something about what the adult is focused on. The infant can use the emotional reaction to decide which object or event the new word is about.
This means infants are not relying on gaze alone. They combine several kinds of information about the speaker’s mind, including where the speaker looks and how the speaker feels, to figure out what a word refers to.
Section 14: Word Learning Strategies and the Whole Object Assumption

Word learning strategies are default strategies that children bring to the word learning situation. A default is the assumption a child makes automatically, unless something in the situation suggests otherwise.
These strategies function to narrow the hypothesis space for new words. Rather than weighing every possible meaning equally, the child starts with built-in guesses about what kinds of meanings words usually have. This cuts down the number of possibilities right away.
The whole object assumption is one such strategy. Under this assumption, a child hearing a new word expects it to refer to the whole object, not to one of its parts or properties.
Applied to the cat example, the whole object assumption leads the child to take “cat” as the name for the entire animal. Meanings such as “leg,” “whiskers,” or “furry” are set aside, at least at first. This removes a large portion of the hypothesis space in a single step.
Section 15: Mutual Exclusivity (Markman, 1989)

Mutual exclusivity, described by Markman (1989), is another word learning strategy. It is the assumption that, as a default, objects can have only one name. If something already has a name, the child does not expect it to take on a second one.
The main implication is that new words map to nameless objects. Suppose a child already knows the word “dog.” The child sees a dog next to an unfamiliar bird, such as a cockatoo, and hears a new word. The dog already has a name, so the child assumes the new word must belong to the bird, which does not yet have a name in the child’s vocabulary.
This strategy lets children learn new words quickly in situations where the speaker gives no other cue. The child can work out the referent simply by ruling out the objects whose names it already knows.
Since mutual exclusivity is a default, it can be overridden. Objects in fact often have more than one name. A dog can also be called an animal or a pet. Children eventually learn these additional names, but their starting assumption is one name per object.
Mutual exclusivity emerges during the second half of the second year. This means it appears sometime between about 18 and 24 months of age.
Section 16: How Children Start Putting Words Together

In production, it can be difficult to tell what is going on when children produce only one, two, or three words at a time. A short utterance can come from very different kinds of knowledge. A two-word phrase might reflect real knowledge of how words combine, or it might be a memorized chunk, or it might just be two words that both happen to matter in the moment.
Menn distinguishes among four kinds of early multi-word speech.
“Just a bunch of relevant words.” Here the child says several words that all relate to the situation, but there is no structure that connects them. The words are linked only because they all fit what is happening.
Formulas. A formula is a phrase the child has stored and uses as a single unit. The child does not build it out of separate words. It is reproduced whole, the way it was heard.
Word combinations. Here the child puts separate words together in a meaningful way. Each word plays its own part in the overall message.
Stable constructions, which are not necessarily adult-like. A construction is a consistent pattern for arranging words. A stable construction is one the child uses reliably across many different utterances, even if the pattern does not yet match the way adults speak.
Three utterances from a child with arms raised make these distinctions concrete: “Up!”, “Pick you up!”, and “Mommy up!”
“Up!” is a single word. On its own, it communicates the child’s request clearly through the context of raised arms.
“Pick you up!” uses “you” to refer to the child herself. This suggests the phrase may be a formula, copied whole from what a parent says, such as “I’ll pick you up.” The child may not be combining “pick,” “you,” and “up” as separate words at all.
“Mommy up!” combines two words. It could be a true word combination, with “Mommy” naming who should act and “up” naming what should happen. But it could also be just two relevant words said together.
A full description of the context in which an utterance is produced is necessary to tell these categories apart, but it is often not sufficient. Knowing what was happening when the child spoke helps us interpret the utterance. Even with complete context, though, it can remain unclear whether a given phrase is a formula, a combination, or a stable construction.
Section 17: Comprehension vs. Production and the Structure-Mapping Account (Gertner and Fisher)

Children at the one-word stage in production can already use basic transitive word order in comprehension. The one-word stage is the period when children speak mostly in single words. A transitive sentence has a verb with two participants, such as “The boy is tickling the girl.” In English, the first noun is usually the one doing the action, and the second noun is the one receiving it. Children who cannot yet produce such sentences can still understand them using this word order. Their comprehension runs ahead of their production.
Gertner and Fisher explain this ability through early syntactic bootstrapping via the structure-mapping account. Syntactic bootstrapping means using the structure of a sentence to help learn the meaning of its words, especially its verbs. The structure-mapping account describes how the child lines up the parts of a sentence with the parts of an observed scene.
The input sentence: The child hears “The boy is tickling the girl!” Even without a full grasp of grammar, the child forms a partial sentence representation. In this representation, the verb “tickle” comes with two nouns: the first noun is “boy” and the second noun is “girl.”
The observed scene: At the same time, the child watches a scene where a boy is tickling a girl and the girl is giggling. From this scene, the child forms several conceptual representations, which are mental descriptions of the events taking place.
One event is giggling, in which the girl is the agent. An agent is the participant who performs an action.
Another event is tickling, in which the boy is the agent and the girl is the patient. A patient is the participant who receives the action.
There are also two playing events, one with the boy as agent and one with the girl as agent.
The mapping: The child links the two representations through a one-to-one mapping between nouns and participant roles. The sentence contains two nouns, so the child looks for an event that has two participants. Among the events in the scene, only tickling involves both an agent and a patient. The giggling and playing events each have only one participant. So the child maps the sentence onto the tickling event, with “boy” as the agent and “girl” as the patient.
This process lets the child use the number of nouns in a sentence to narrow down what a verb could mean. The child can do this before being able to produce such sentences.
Section 18: A Generalization Test (Gertner et al., 2006)

A generalization test checks whether children have truly learned the transitive structure. If children have really learned that the first noun is the agent and the second is the patient, they will generalize this pattern to new verbs. Generalizing means applying a known pattern to items the child has never encountered.
Gertner et al. (2006) tested this using a made-up verb, “gorp.”
Setup: Two-year-olds first heard the sentence “Hey look! The duck’s gonna gorp the bunny!” while the screen was blank. Then they saw two videos side by side, each showing a different novel action. In one video, the duck was acting on the bunny. In the other, the bunny was acting on the duck. While the videos played, the children heard “The duck is gorping the bunny!” A second test trial followed the same pattern with the instruction “Find gorping!”
What the task requires: “Gorp” is a new word, and both actions are new, so the child cannot rely on knowing the verb or the action. To choose the picture that matches “The duck is gorping the bunny,” the child must know that the first noun names the one doing the action. The child must then find the video in which the duck is the agent and the bunny is the patient.
Observation: Two-year-olds looked significantly longer at the duck acting on the rabbit than at the rabbit acting on the duck.
Interpretation: The children applied transitive word order to a verb they had never heard before. This means their knowledge of the structure is general. It is not tied to specific verbs they had already learned.
This comprehension ability far exceeds what children this age can produce. At this age, children typically have a mean length of utterance (MLU) of around 2. MLU is the average number of morphemes in a child’s utterances. A morpheme is the smallest unit of language that carries meaning, such as a word like “duck” or an ending like the “-ing” in “gorping.”
With an MLU around 2, a child’s longer utterances of three morphemes might sound like “Duck gorp bunny.” This leaves out words like “the” and “is” and the “-ing” ending.
Yet these same children can understand the full sentence “The duck is gorping the bunny” and match it correctly to the right action. Their understanding of sentence structure is well ahead of what they can say themselves.
