Section 1: Babies Are Overrated

Sensitive periods for a second language (L2) are the idea that there is a window early in life when language learning comes most easily. People often summarize this by saying that infants, toddlers, and children are simply “better” at learning language than adults. That claim needs care. Children do have an advantage, but it is a narrower advantage than the everyday version of the claim suggests.
Consider what a typical child can do after about 18 months of exposure to their first language. By 18 months, a child uses at least 20 words. If an adult took 18 months of language classes and came out knowing only about 20 words, everyone would call that a failure. Measured by early progress, infants are slow learners.
Other typical 18-month milestones are just as modest. The child understands “in and out” and “off and on.” They point to several body parts when asked, and they answer simple questions such as “Where’s teddy?” or “What’s that?” with words or gestures.
The child also shows some pretend play, such as giving teddy a drink. They make at least four different consonant sounds, such as b, n, d, g, w, and h. They enjoy being read to, and they point to pictures with one finger.
Adults initially learn more quickly than children. Over the first stretch of learning, an adult picks up vocabulary and grammar faster than a young child does. There are two main reasons. The first is transfer from the first language (L1), meaning the adult can reuse what they already know about language. The second is that adults have better general cognitive skills, such as memory, attention, and reasoning.
Adults fall behind children in ultimate attainment, which is the final level of skill a learner reaches after many years. Adults rarely become native-like in a second language, while children exposed early usually do. Transfer may be part of the reason for this too. The same L1 knowledge that speeds up early learning may later hold the learner back from fully native performance.

Section 2: Language Milestones as General Conceptual Abilities

Some of the items listed as “language milestones” are not purely about language. Understanding “in and out” and “off and on” depends on grasping spatial relations between objects, that is, knowing how one object can be inside, outside, on top of, or off of another. That is a general conceptual ability that the child must build before the words can mean anything.
Pretend play, such as giving teddy a drink, depends on event representations. To act out this scene, the child has to understand an event with a giver, a receiver, and an object being given. Again, this is general understanding of the world, not just knowledge of words.
An older second-language learner already has these concepts in place. Adults understand spatial relations and events well before they start a new language. So the adult’s task is smaller. They can focus on learning how those concepts are expressed in the target language, instead of building the concepts from scratch.
Adult learners also bring something else to the task. They already know another language. That prior language affects how the second language is learned, sometimes in helpful ways and sometimes in harmful ways.

Section 3: Transfer

Transfer is the use of native language (L1) processes and representations for a second language (L2). In other words, learners bring the sound categories, grammar, and vocabulary of their first language into the new one. They often do this without noticing.
Transfer can have both positive and negative effects on second-language learning and performance.
Positive transfer happens when the L1 and L2 work the same way, so old knowledge gives the learner a head start.
Negative transfer happens when the two languages differ, so old knowledge leads the learner to the wrong answer.
A sentence like “Do you not be happy with me as the translator of the books of you?” is a classic case of negative transfer in grammar. Each word is English, but the way the words are arranged follows another language’s patterns. The question structure and the phrase “the books of you” come from carrying L1 sentence structure straight into English. The result is understandable but clearly not native.

Section 4: Transfer and Phonetic/Phonological Learning

Earlier topics in the course covered categorical perception, which is the tendency to hear speech sounds as belonging to distinct categories. A related idea is acquired similarity. When two sounds fall into the same category in your native language, learning that language trains you to treat them as more alike. Over time, those sounds become harder to tell apart.
Hindi offers a clear example through its minimal pairs. A minimal pair is two words that differ in only one sound, where that one sound changes the meaning. Hindi separates dental consonants, made with the tongue against the teeth, from retroflex consonants, made with the tongue tip curled back toward the roof of the mouth.
With a dental t, the word tap means “hot.” With a retroflex t, tap means “a kind of beat.”
With a dental d, dab means “pressure.” With a retroflex d, dab means “a kind of fruit.”
Ari with a plain r means “saw,” the noun. With a retroflex sound in the middle, it means “crooked,” in its feminine adjective form.
English has neither dental nor retroflex stops as separate categories. Instead, English uses an alveolar category, made with the tongue on the ridge just behind the upper teeth. Native English speakers tend to hear both the Hindi dental and the Hindi retroflex sounds as their own English alveolar sound.
Both Hindi sounds get placed into one English category.
Once that happens, acquired similarity makes the two sounds seem nearly the same.
The learner then struggles to hear and produce the difference that Hindi uses to separate meanings.
In this case, transfer makes learning the L2 contrast more difficult. The English listener’s old category gets in the way of building two new ones. Recordings of these sounds, and of sounds from many other languages, are available in the UCLA Phonetics Archive.

Section 5: Transfer and Grammar Learning: Classifiers

Classifiers are a grammatical feature that is somewhat unusual across the world’s languages. A classifier is a word that must appear between a number or demonstrative and a noun. The classifier chosen depends on what kind of thing the noun refers to.
In Mandarin Chinese, the pattern is a demonstrative or number, then a nominal classifier, then the noun. For example, yī gè rén means “one person.” Here yī is “one,” gè is the classifier, and rén is “person.”
Different classifiers go with different kinds of nouns, and many of them track the shape or type of the object.
Gè is the general classifier, used broadly.
Bǎ is used with objects that have a handle. Běn is used with books and similar objects.
Kuài is used with objects divided into pieces. Liàng is used with wheeled vehicles, and tái with devices and gadgets.
Xiē marks an indeterminate amount, or the plural of pronouns and demonstrative adjectives.
Zhāng is used with flat objects, and zhī with long, thin objects.
Native English speakers learning Chinese often have difficulty learning classifiers. In English, when we say “a chair,” “that dog,” “the car,” or “one book,” we do not think about what kind of object the noun names when choosing “a,” “that,” “the,” or “one.” The same small words work for every noun.
Chinese is different. Each noun requires its own classifier, and the right choice depends on the noun’s meaning, such as whether the object is flat, long and thin, or has a handle.
One way to explain the difficulty is that English speakers have learned to ignore the cues that would predict classifiers. Properties like flatness or having a handle never mattered for English grammar. Part of learning English was learning to pay no attention to them when choosing words like “the” or “that.” That learned habit transfers into Chinese and makes the classifier system harder to pick up.

Section 6: Transfer and Grammatical Gender

Grammatical gender is another area where transfer causes problems. Grammatical gender is a system that sorts nouns into classes, such as masculine and feminine in French. It is different from social or cultural gender. There is nothing “male” about a book or “female” about liberty. Yet in French, le livre, “the book,” is masculine, and la liberté, “liberty,” is feminine.
Whether grammatical gender has any effect on how speakers think about objects is a question for the later topic of language and thought.
French nouns can sometimes be sorted by their endings. Some endings tend to go with masculine nouns, and others with feminine nouns.
Common masculine endings include -age, as in le fromage, “the cheese”; -r, as in le professeur, “the teacher”; -t, as in le chat, “the cat”; and -isme, as in le capitalisme.
Common feminine endings include -ie, as in la boulangerie, “the bakery”; -ion, as in la nation; -ité, as in la fraternité, “brotherhood”; -nce, as in la balance, “the scales”; and endings like -nne, -mme, and -lle, as in la fille, “the girl,” and l’indienne, “the Indian woman.”
Many common nouns give no such clue. Le cheval, “the horse,” le chien, “the dog,” and le bruit, “the noise,” are masculine. La colombe, “the dove,” la chemise, “the shirt,” and la maison, “the house,” are feminine. These simply have to be memorized.
Grammatical gender matters because of agreement. Pronouns, adjectives, some verb forms, and other words must all match the gender of the noun they go with. So a single wrong gender choice spreads into errors across the whole sentence.
French is famous for synonyms that differ in gender. Pairs such as auto and voiture, rivière and fleuve, and vélo and bicyclette have nearly the same meaning, yet the members of a pair can belong to different gender classes. Gender cannot be predicted from what the noun means. For an English speaker, whose language has no gender on nouns like these, there is no L1 knowledge to help, and the whole system must be built from the ground up.

Section 7: Positive Transfer: Cognates and Loan Words

Transfer is not all bad. Vocabulary learning in some pairs of languages is helped by the similarity of words across languages. When a new L2 word sounds or looks like a word the learner already knows, both its form and its meaning are easier to learn.
This similarity has two main sources.
Sometimes it comes from shared etymology, meaning both words descend from the same older word. Words related this way are called cognates. The French words auto, rivière, and bicyclette resemble the English “auto,” “river,” and “bicycle,” so an English speaker can guess their meanings.
Sometimes it comes from more recent borrowing, where one language takes a word directly from another. These are called loan words. Japanese korokke comes from “croquette,” and monorēru comes from “monorail.” An English speaker who hears these Japanese words can often recognize them.

Section 8: False Cognates

Not all similar-sounding words are cognates. Some words look or sound alike across two languages but have unrelated meanings. These are often called false cognates or “false friends.”
The bias to interpret an L2 word as related to a similar-sounding L1 word can be very strong. Learners reach for the familiar meaning automatically.
Sometimes the result is embarrassing. In Spanish, embarazado means “pregnant,” not “embarrassed.” An English speaker who tries to say “Arnold is embarrassed” by saying “Arnold está embarazado” has actually said “Arnold is pregnant.” The mistake comes directly from negative transfer, since the English word “embarrassed” pulls the learner toward the wrong meaning.

Section 9: Transfer in the Lab: The De Groot and Keijzer Design

A lab study can test whether people really use cognates when learning a new language. De Groot and Keijzer (2000), published in Language Learning, used a paired associate learning task. In this kind of task, participants learn pairs of items so that one item later brings the other to mind.
The participants were Dutch speakers, and they learned made-up words as stand-ins for words in a “second language.” The made-up words varied in two ways.
They varied in how similar they were to their Dutch partner word. Similar pairs acted as artificial cognates, and dissimilar pairs acted as non-cognates.
They also varied in concreteness. Concrete words name things you can see or touch, while abstract words name ideas.
These two factors were fully crossed, so every combination appeared.
Gitaar paired with gettar means “guitar.” It is concrete and a cognate.
Tijger paired with duunze means “tiger.” It is concrete but a non-cognate.
Domein paired with domaan means “domain.” It is abstract and a cognate.
Herstel paired with groop means “repair.” It is abstract and a non-cognate.
The procedure was simple. Participants saw pairs of Dutch words and their made-up partners and were asked to learn the pairs. They were then tested again a week later to measure retention, meaning how much they still remembered.

Section 10: De Groot and Keijzer Results: Cognates Help Learning and Memory

Recall was measured across four sessions. Sessions 1 through 3 were learning sessions. Session 4 came one week later, with no training in between, so it measured how well the words were kept in memory.
Cognates were learned more rapidly. In Session 1, concrete cognates were recalled about 70% of the time, while concrete non-cognates were recalled only about 44% of the time. For abstract words, the gap was similar, with about 43% for cognates against 19% for non-cognates. From the very first session, word similarity gave learners a large head start.
Cognates were also remembered more easily. After the week with no training, concrete cognates were still recalled about 78% of the time, compared with 69% for concrete non-cognates. For abstract words, the difference was much larger. Abstract cognates stayed at about 68%, while abstract non-cognates fell to 36%.
Response times pointed the same way. In every session, people answered faster for cognates than for non-cognates. Concrete words were also answered faster than abstract words.
The result is evidence that cognate status can contribute to the very earliest stages of word learning in a second language. Similarity to a known word speeds up learning in the first session, and it protects the new word from being forgotten.
Concreteness had a large effect as well. Concrete words were learned faster and remembered better than abstract ones. One account of why concrete words have this advantage goes by the name “context availability.”

Section 11: Is This a Good Experiment? Receptive vs. Productive Testing

A fair concern is how much of this “learning” really happened in the lab. Many of the concrete cognate pairs are very close in form. Examples include broer and breur, brood and brodde, lepel and lippel, naald and nadel, paraplu and parredu, rivier and riffer, trein and trinen, varken and vark, viool and vialo, and vrouw and vrauk. In many of these, the made-up word keeps most of the sounds of the Dutch word.
Suppose the study had tested only receptive vocabulary, measured here as how easily participants could produce the Dutch word when given its made-up partner. That would not be very impressive. Given gettar, it is easy to come up with gitaar just because the two look alike. Even English speakers might guess the meanings of gitaar, tijger, and domein without any Dutch training. So a cognate advantage on a receptive test could come from guessing, not from learning.
The study also included a test of expressive vocabulary, which the authors called productive testing. Here participants had to produce the made-up word itself when given the Dutch word. Guessing helps much less here, since the participant must recall the exact new form, such as gettar or duunze.

Section 12: What Participants Actually Learned

The two kinds of testing can be compared directly. Each was measured right after learning, in Session 3, and again a week later, in Session 4. The difference between the two sessions is the forgetting effect, meaning how many percentage points were lost over the week.
In productive testing, cognates still had a clear advantage. Right after learning, cognates were recalled about 90% of the time, and non-cognates about 82%. After a week, cognates dropped to about 73%, while non-cognates dropped to about 53%.
Cognates lost about 16 points over the week.
Non-cognates lost about 30 points, nearly twice as much.
Concrete words lost about 19 points, while abstract words lost about 27.
In receptive testing, scores were higher overall, which fits with this test being easier. Right after learning, cognates were near ceiling at about 99%, and non-cognates were at about 91%. A week later, cognates were still at about 91%, but non-cognates had fallen to 63%.
Cognates lost only about 8 points.
Non-cognates lost about 28 points.
Concrete words lost about 9 points, and abstract words lost about 27.
Several conclusions follow from these numbers.
The cognate advantage appears in productive testing too. Participants had to produce the new made-up form, so they really did learn the new words, not just guess the Dutch ones.
Cognates were forgotten less than non-cognates on both kinds of test.
Non-cognates were forgotten by about the same amount on both tests, close to 30 points. Cognates, by contrast, held up best on the receptive test, where their similarity to the Dutch word could still help at retrieval. So part of the cognate advantage on the receptive test reflects that easy route back to the known Dutch word.

Section 13: Are Similar L1 Words Always Activated When Processing L2?

A related research question asks whether similar L1 words are always activated whenever someone processes their second language. In other words, when a bilingual hears a word in L2, does the sound of that word also wake up L1 words that sound alike? There have been many studies of this question.
One clear example is a “visual world” experiment by Marian and Spivey (2003), published in Bilingualism: Language and Cognition.

Section 14: The Visual World Paradigm

The visual world paradigm was introduced earlier in the course, in the discussion of categorical perception. Participants see a small set of pictures and hear a spoken instruction about one of them. Their eye movements are tracked as the word unfolds.
Eye movements tell us about competition between the possible responses that a participant is considering. If a listener briefly looks at a picture that is not the target, that picture’s word was probably active in their mind for a moment.
In the McMurray, Tanenhaus, Aslin, and Spivey (2003) study, each display had a target such as a pear, a competitor such as a bear, and two unrelated fillers, such as a lamp and a ship. “Pear” and “bear” differ only in whether the first sound is voiced.
The amount of competition between BEAR and PEAR depended on distance from the category boundary.
When the first sound was close to the boundary between /b/ and /p/, listeners looked at the competitor more.
When it was far from the boundary, they looked at it less.
Listeners were sensitive to small sound differences inside a category, even though they still heard the sound as one category.

Section 15: The Cohort Effect

Allopenna et al. (1994), published in the Journal of Memory and Language, used the same eye-tracking method to study the cohort effect. A word’s cohort is the set of words that begin with the same sounds. “Beaker” and “beetle” are in the same cohort, since they both start with “bee.”
Participants saw a grid of objects and were told to pick up and move one of them. In a typical trial, the display included four kinds of pictures.
The referent was the target, such as a beaker.
The cohort competitor began with the same sounds, such as a beetle.
The rhyme competitor ended with the same sounds, such as a speaker.
The unrelated item shared no sounds, such as a carriage.
Looks to the different items followed a clear pattern over time.
Looks to the beaker and the beetle rose together at first. While only “bee” had been heard, both words fit the input, so listeners considered both.
Looks to the beetle peaked roughly half a second after the word began and then dropped. Once the later sounds ruled it out, listeners stopped considering it.
Looks to the beaker kept rising until nearly every trial ended with the eyes on the beaker.
The rhyme competitor drew a smaller, later rise in looks. The unrelated item drew almost none.
Looks to the cohort competitor began before the end of the spoken word. Listeners do not wait for a word to finish. They activate every candidate that matches the input so far, and those candidates compete.

Section 16: Marian and Spivey: Cross-Language Competitors

Marian and Spivey tested the cohort effect with cross-language competitors. Their participants were native Russian speakers who also spoke English. The question was whether a word in one language would activate similar-sounding words in the other language.
Each target word was paired with two kinds of competitors.
A within-language competitor came from the same language as the target and began with similar sounds.
A between-language competitor came from the other language and also began with similar sounds.
For Russian targets, a typical set looked like this. The target spichki means “matches.” The English competitor was “spear,” which starts with similar sounds. The Russian competitor was spitsi, meaning “knitting needles.” Other sets used targets such as marka, “stamp,” with the English competitor “marker,” and platye, “dress,” with the English competitor “plum.”
For English targets, the logic was reversed. The target “speaker” was paired with the English competitor “spear” and the Russian competitor spichki, meaning “matches.” Other sets included “marker” with marka, “stamp,” and “plug” with platye, “dress.”
The hypothesis follows from the cohort effect. If L1 words are always activated, then a native Russian speaker who hears the English word “speaker” should briefly activate spichki. That activation should draw their eyes toward the picture of matches, even though the task is entirely in English.

Section 17: Competition During L2 Spoken Word Recognition

In Experiment 1, the native Russian speakers did the task in English, their L2. The measure was the percent of trials on which participants made eye movements to each competitor picture. Each competitor was compared with a control filler object, an unrelated picture shown when no competitor was present. Looks to the filler give a baseline for how often people glance at a picture just by chance.
When only one competitor was on the screen, participants looked at the two kinds of competitors to the same extent.
The between-language competitor, a Russian word like spichki when the target was “speaker,” drew looks on about 18% of trials.
The within-language competitor, an English word like “spear,” also drew looks on about 18% of trials.
The control filler drew looks on only about 7% of trials.
So while listening to English, these bilinguals activated similar-sounding Russian words just as strongly as similar-sounding English words.
When both competitors appeared together, participants looked at the within-language competitor more, on about 19% of trials. They still looked at the between-language competitor a lot, on about 13% of trials. That is still roughly twice as often as the control filler. Even with an English option available, the Russian word kept competing.
In this direction of the task, the answer to the research question is yes. When native Russian speakers process English, similar L1 words become active and compete for recognition.

Section 18: Competition During L1 Spoken Word Recognition

In Experiment 2, the same kind of bilinguals did the task in Russian, their L1. Now the between-language competitors were English words, and the within-language competitors were Russian words.
The pattern was different. English words did not seem to be activated when native Russian speakers did the experiment in Russian.
The within-language Russian competitor drew looks on about 14% of trials when shown alone, and about 13% when shown with the other competitor.
The between-language English competitor drew looks on only about 8% of trials, whether it was shown alone or with the Russian competitor.
The control filler drew looks on about 5% of trials.
The small difference between the between-language competitor at 8% and the control at 5% is not statistically significant. So there is no reliable evidence that English words competed at all during Russian listening.
Together, the two experiments reveal an asymmetry. Listening in the second language activates the first language. Listening in the first language does not reliably activate the second language.

Section 19: The Revised Hierarchical Model of Kroll and Stewart

The Revised Hierarchical Model of Kroll and Stewart (1990; 1994) explains L1 and L2 interactions in terms of spreading activation. Spreading activation is the idea that when one representation in memory becomes active, some of that activity flows along its connections to related representations. The model has three stores: an L1 lexicon, an L2 lexicon, and a shared store of concepts. A lexicon is a mental dictionary of the words a person knows.
The L1 lexicon is assumed to be larger than the L2 lexicon. A person knows more words in their native language, so the L1 store is bigger.
The L1 lexicon is also assumed to be more strongly connected to relevant concepts. The link between L1 words and their meanings is strong in both directions. The link between L2 words and concepts is weak.
The two lexicons are connected to each other by lexical links, and these links are uneven.
The L2 lexicon has strong forward connections to the L1 lexicon. Activation spreads easily from an L2 word to its L1 translation.
The connection from L1 words to L2 words is weaker. Activation spreads less readily in that direction.
The forward direction is stronger as a result of how second languages are typically learned. Learners often study an L2 word by linking it to its L1 translation, such as learning that gato means “cat.” That direction is emphasized in learning, so the L2-to-L1 link becomes strong.

Section 20: Applying the Model to the Marian and Spivey Results

The model can account for what happened when native Russian speakers did the task in their L2, English. The key is that activation passes through Russian, the L1, which has strong links to concepts. The process unfolds in several connected steps.
The picture of matches on the screen activates the concept for “matches.”
That concept partially activates the Russian word for matches, spichki. The link from concepts to L1 words is strong, so this activation is substantial.
The incoming English word “speaker” begins with sounds that overlap with spichki, which adds to the activity of that Russian word.
This activity feeds back to the conceptual representation of “matches,” making the concept even more active.
The strong conceptual activation for “matches” then pulls the listener’s eye movements toward the L1 competitor, the picture of matches.
When the task is done in the L1, Russian, the same loop would have to run through the English words. The conceptual links for L2 are too weak to produce this kind of interference reliably. An English word like “spear” receives little activation from its concept and sends little activation back. So the English competitor does not draw extra looks, which matches the Experiment 2 results.

Section 21: Competition, Interference, and Bilingualism

The model makes a counter-intuitive prediction about balanced bilinguals. A balanced bilingual is someone who is roughly equally skilled in both languages. One might expect such a person to act like “two monolinguals in one,” with each language working on its own without interference.
The model predicts the opposite. In a balanced bilingual, the conceptual links are similarly strong for both languages. That means every concept strongly activates words in both languages at once.
Words from the two languages are therefore active together all the time.
These active words compete with each other for selection.
To use one language, the speaker must engage inhibition, meaning they must suppress the words from the other language.
So competitive dynamics and inhibition are engaged constantly, not just occasionally.
This prediction has led to a large body of research on bilingual advantages in non-linguistic cognitive control tasks. Cognitive control is the ability to focus on what is relevant and suppress what is not. The reasoning is that if bilinguals practice inhibition every time they speak, that constant practice might strengthen cognitive control in general, even on tasks that have nothing to do with language.

Section 22: A Different Learning Sequence: When L1 Use Stops

The research so far has assumed one particular sequence of second-language learning. A person begins learning their L1. Later, they start learning an L2. Throughout that time, they continue learning and using the L1. Both languages stay active in the person’s life.
A different situation arises when a person stops using the L1 after the L2 is introduced. The L1 is learned early, but then it disappears from the environment, and only the L2 is used from then on. The question is what happens to the first language when it is no longer heard or spoken.

Section 23: Pallier et al.: Can a Second Language Replace the First?

Pallier, Dehaene, Poline, LeBihan, Argenti, Dupoux, and Mehler studied this question in a paper titled “Brain Imaging of Language Plasticity in Adopted Adults: Can a Second Language Replace the First?” The researchers worked in Paris and Orsay, France.
The participants were adults aged 20 to 32 who had been adopted into French homes from Korean orphanages between the ages of 3 and 8. These adoptees had heard and spoken Korean as young children. After adoption, they lived in French-speaking homes and stopped using Korean.
The researchers tested whether these adults had any residual knowledge of Korean left. They used both behavioral tasks and brain imaging.

Section 24: The Korean Three-Way Stop Contrast

One useful test of leftover Korean knowledge is the Korean three-way stop contrast. A stop is a consonant made by fully blocking the airflow and then releasing it, as in p, t, and k. Korean separates three kinds of stops at each place in the mouth.
Aspirated stops are released with a strong puff of air.
Plain stops are produced without that strong puff.
Tense stops are produced with a tighter, stiffer quality.
Each place of articulation has a set of three words that differ only in which type of stop begins the word.
In the bilabial series, made with both lips, pʰul means “grass,” pul means “fire,” and the tense version means “horn.”
In the dental series, tʰal means “mask,” tal means “moon,” and the tense version means “daughter.”
In the velar series, made with the back of the tongue against the soft palate, kʰo means “nose,” kok means “music,” and the tense version means “exactly.”
French does not use this three-way distinction. So a person who had kept any Korean sound categories should be better than a native French speaker at telling these stops apart. These recordings, like the Hindi ones, come from the UCLA Phonetics Archive.

Section 25: Ventureyra et al.: Phonetic Discrimination in Adoptees

Ventureyra et al. tested three groups on a phonetic discrimination task. The groups were Korean adoptees in France, native French speakers, and native Korean speakers. Participants heard pairs of sounds and judged whether they were the same or different. The measure was the percentage of “different” responses for each type of contrast.
The contrasts included three stop contrasts for p and k: plain versus tense, plain versus aspirated, and tense versus aspirated. There was also a plain versus tense contrast for s, a vowel contrast, and a set of “same” trials where the two sounds were identical.
The native Korean speakers heard the consonant contrasts very well. They responded “different” on most trials for every Korean consonant contrast.
The Korean adoptees were no better than monolingual French speakers at these contrasts. Both groups responded “different” on only a small fraction of trials. For the plain versus tense stop contrast, both groups were close to the floor.
The two control conditions confirm that the task itself worked. On the vowel contrast, all three groups said “different” almost every time, so everyone was paying attention and could hear clear differences. On the same trials, all groups rarely said “different,” so no group was simply answering “different” at random. The adoptees’ failure was specific to the Korean consonant contrasts.

Section 26: Pallier et al.: Behavioral Tests for Any Trace of Korean

In a more extensive study, Pallier et al. gave adoptees and monolingual French speakers several more tasks. The adoptees were no better than monolingual francophones on any of them.
The first task was distinguishing Korean from a collection of largely unrelated languages. Participants heard sentences in Korean, Japanese, Polish, Swedish, and Wolof, and rated how likely each was to be Korean on a scale from 1 to 7.
Both groups gave higher ratings to Korean and Japanese sentences than to Polish, Swedish, or Wolof sentences.
Critically, both groups rated Korean and Japanese about equally. Neither group could pick out Korean from Japanese.
The adoptees’ ratings matched those of the French speakers, so they showed no special ability to recognize Korean.
The second task was identifying the correct Korean translation of French words. Both groups recognized the correct translation about half of the time, with no reliable difference between them.
The third task was speech segment detection. Participants heard a recorded sentence and then judged whether a short excerpt had been repeated from it.
Both groups did best with French sentences.
With Korean, Japanese, and Polish sentences, both groups did equally well and slightly worse than with French.
The adoptees showed no advantage for Korean over the other foreign languages.
These are not standard psycholinguistic tasks. Pallier et al. designed them to catch any remaining trace of Korean, even a very faint one. They did not find any.

Section 27: Pallier et al.: Brain Imaging Results

Pallier et al. also used brain imaging while participants listened to sentences in different languages. They then subtracted the activity for one language from the activity for another. This subtraction reveals which brain regions respond more to one language than the other.
Listening to French activated the classical language network in both groups. This network includes superior temporal regions, along the top of the temporal lobe, and inferior frontal regions, in the lower part of the frontal lobe.
The comparisons were French minus Polish, French minus Japanese, and French minus Korean. Polish and Japanese were languages that neither group knew, so they serve as baselines.
The key comparison was French minus Korean. If the adoptees had any residual processing ability for Korean, Korean should have activated their language network more than an unknown language would. In that case, subtracting Korean from French would leave a smaller difference for adoptees than for French speakers.
That did not happen. The Korean adoptees’ French-minus-Korean result looked like their French-minus-Polish and French-minus-Japanese results.
Their brains responded to Korean the same way they responded to languages they had never heard.

Section 28: Competitive Dynamics and Monocular Deprivation

L2 and L1 interactions can also be understood in terms of competitive dynamics in the brain. Suppose the L1 is represented as a set of connections within and between different brain regions. If those connections are never activated again, they could be “taken over” by the L2.
Monocular deprivation in mice is a well-studied case of this kind of takeover. In monocular deprivation, one eye is covered so that it sends no visual input. Neurons in the visual brain normally receive input from both eyes, and those inputs compete for connection sites called dendritic spines. Once one eye is covered, the changes unfold in stages.
After 2 to 3 days, there is increased spine motility. The spines start moving and changing shape, and the connections become unstable.
After 3 to 4 days, there is spine loss and Wallerian degeneration. Wallerian degeneration is the breakdown of an axon that has lost its function. The connections from the covered eye start to fall apart.
After more than a week, there is axon sprouting and de novo spine birth, meaning brand-new spines form. The input from the open eye grows into the space that the covered eye left behind and forms new connections there.
The same logic may apply to a language that stops being used. The L1 connections, like the covered eye’s input, receive no more activity. The L2, like the open eye, keeps receiving input and may claim the neural resources the L1 once used.

Section 29: Can a Second Language Replace the First?

Pallier et al. asked whether a second language can replace the first. Their answer was, apparently, yes. Korean adoptees raised in France showed no detectable Korean knowledge in behavior or in brain activity.
That leaves a further question. Even if no trace of the L1 can be detected, there might still be traces of L1 left behind that can be “salvaged.” These traces might not show up on a test, but they might make the language easier to relearn later.

Section 30: Au et al.: Overhearing a Language During Childhood

More recent work by Terry Au’s group at UCLA, published in Psychological Science under the title “Overhearing a Language During Childhood,” examined this question. The authors were Terry Kit-fong Au, Leah Knightly, Sun-Ah Jun, and Janet Oh.
The participants were undergraduate students who had overheard Spanish frequently as children but never learned to speak it. They heard Spanish around them growing up but did not use it themselves.
All participants started classroom Spanish instruction at around age 14. They were tested at the end of a Year 2 college Spanish course. The researchers compared three groups.
Native speakers of Spanish.
Childhood overhearers, who heard Spanish as children.
Typical late L2 learners, who had no childhood exposure and learned Spanish only in class.
Childhood slang tests checked whether overhearers knew informal Spanish words that children typically hear.
On production, natives scored about 79%, overhearers about 17%, and late learners about 3%.
On comprehension, natives scored about 88%, overhearers about 22%, and late learners about 3%.
Overhearers knew far less than natives, but they still knew reliably more than late learners.
Voice onset time, or VOT, is the time between the release of a stop consonant and the start of vocal fold vibration. It is one of the main sound cues that separates voiced from voiceless stops.
For voiceless stops at the start of words, natives and overhearers both produced a VOT of about 19 to 20 milliseconds. Late learners produced a much longer VOT, about 36 milliseconds.
For voiceless stops in the middle of words, the pattern was the same. Natives and overhearers were around 22 to 23 milliseconds, and late learners were around 31.
The overhearers’ VOT was not reliably different from the natives’. Their pronunciation of these sounds was native-like.
Lenition is the softening of a consonant, where a voiced stop is produced more like a smooth, continuous sound instead of a full stop. Native Spanish speakers often lenite voiced stops.
At the start of words, natives lenited about 43% of voiced stops, overhearers about 24%, and late learners about 1%.
In the middle of words, natives lenited about 72%, overhearers about 63%, and late learners about 23%.
For word-medial stops, overhearers were not reliably different from natives, while late learners lenited far less.
Accent ratings were given on a 5-point scale, with higher ratings meaning more native-like pronunciation.
For voiceless stops, natives were rated about 4.3, overhearers about 3.5, and late learners about 2.2.
For voiced stops, natives were rated about 4.4, overhearers about 3.3, and late learners about 2.7.
Overhearers sounded more native-like than late learners, though not fully native.
Grammar told a different story. On morphology production and grammaticality judgments, the overhearers did no better than the late learners.
On gender agreement, natives scored about 93%, while overhearers and late learners were both around 70%.
On grammaticality judgment accuracy, natives scored about 94%, while overhearers and late learners were both around 63 to 64%.
Both learner groups were also much slower than natives to make grammaticality judgments.
So childhood overhearing helped with pronunciation, and to some degree with childhood vocabulary, but not with grammar.
These differences were tested statistically with Tukey’s HSD test, a method for comparing several group means while controlling for chance. Groups marked as reliably different met a threshold of p < .05.

Section 31: Bringing the Adoptee and Overhearer Data Together

Combining the Korean adoptee data with the overhearer data leads to two conclusions.
If the L1 is removed from the environment entirely, the L2 can appear to completely replace it. The Korean adoptees showed no measurable trace of Korean.
This replacement is not necessarily complete. There may be residual knowledge that can be “unmasked” with later learning. The overhearers’ early exposure did not show up as speaking ability, yet it gave them a clear pronunciation advantage once they started studying Spanish.
Several important differences between these two populations make them hard to compare directly.
Korean is rarely heard or encountered in France. Spanish, by contrast, is heard constantly in Los Angeles. So the overhearers may have kept hearing Spanish throughout their lives, while the adoptees heard almost no Korean after adoption.
A “heritage language” spoken by other family members is different from a language not spoken by anyone you know. Many overhearers likely heard Spanish from relatives, while the adoptees had no Korean speakers around them.
Cognates differ across the language pairs. Spanish and English share many cognates, while Korean and French share very few.
Spanish and English share a writing system, while Korean and French do not.
This is a general issue in research on second-language acquisition and bilingualism. Languages and learning situations differ in many, many ways. These differences are very hard, and perhaps impossible, to control in an experiment. So any comparison between two groups of learners has to consider all of the ways their languages and life situations differ.
