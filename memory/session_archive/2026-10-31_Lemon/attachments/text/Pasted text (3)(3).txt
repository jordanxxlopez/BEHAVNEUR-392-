Section 1: Nature and Nurture in Child Language

Nativist positions on child language build on Chomsky’s critique of Skinner. The central claim is that the most important feature of language is its generativity. Speakers can produce and understand sentences they have never heard before, so language cannot be a stored list of sentences that were learned one at a time.
On this view, acquiring a language means identifying the abstract grammar that produces the sentences of that language. A child is not memorizing sentences. A child is working out the system that generates them.
Nativists argue that the general learning mechanisms studied by behaviorists were not up to this task. They give two reasons.
Grammatical and ungrammatical sentences are not “similar” in any simple way. A learning mechanism that works by generalizing from similarity would have no easy basis for separating allowed sentences from disallowed ones.
The context in which children acquire language does not look like operant conditioning. In operant conditioning, behavior changes because of rewards and punishments that depend on what the learner does. For language acquisition, these reward and punishment contingencies do not hold.
Nativist approaches instead assert two things.
The first is innate knowledge of what language is like, which is called Universal Grammar. The child begins with knowledge about the general form that human languages take.
The second is language-specific acquisition processes. These are learning mechanisms dedicated to language rather than shared with other kinds of learning.
The argument rests on a fact about children. All normal children acquire essentially comparable grammars of great complexity with remarkable rapidity. This suggests that human beings are somehow specially designed to do this. The design includes a data-handling, or “hypothesis-formulating,” ability of unknown character and complexity.
Section 2: Categorical Perception

Categorical perception is a pattern in which listeners treat sounds that change gradually as members of distinct categories. The classic demonstration used a series of synthetic syllables that differed in small, equal acoustic steps. The steps were changes in the formant transition, the quick movement of the formant at the start of the syllable. Along this series, listeners hear the syllables as b, then d, then g.
In the labeling (identification) data, listeners do not hear a gradual blend from one consonant to the next. Instead, the labels switch abruptly from one category to another. Within each category, nearly every stimulus receives the same label. The change in labeling happens over only a step or two.
The discrimination data come from tasks in which listeners judge whether two stimuli are the same or different. The two stimuli are separated by one, two, or three steps along the series.
Accuracy is low when both stimuli come from the same category, even though they differ acoustically.
Accuracy is high when the two stimuli fall on opposite sides of a category boundary.
The predicted discrimination curve is the performance expected if listeners could only tell stimuli apart by their labels. The obtained curve follows these predicted peaks closely. Listeners are therefore sensitive to differences that cross a category boundary and relatively insensitive to equal-sized differences inside a category.
Early theories treated categorical perception as language-specific and species-specific. Only humans were known to show it, and only for speech sounds.
Categorical perception is a compelling example of how similarity between stimuli is defined in linguistic terms rather than physical terms. Two pairs of sounds can be equally far apart acoustically. One pair sounds identical because both sounds belong to one category. The other pair sounds different because the sounds belong to two categories.
Section 3: Categorical Perception in Infants: Methods

Testing infants requires methods that do not depend on spoken answers. Two methods are used in the studies covered here.
The conditioned head turn procedure is associated with Janet Werker. The infant is trained to turn the head when a change in the sound occurs. The head turn is the response that indicates the infant has detected a difference between sounds.
High amplitude sucking records how strongly and how often an infant sucks. The sucking rate is tracked over time, so changes in interest can be measured as changes in sucking. The pictured trace of sucking bursts comes from an unrelated paper by Rochat and Striano (2001). It is included only as an example of the recording method.
Section 4: Eimas et al. (1971): Categorical Perception in Four-Month-Old Infants

Voice onset time (VOT) is the cue that separates sounds such as /b/ from /p/, or /d/ from /t/. It is the delay between the release of the consonant and the start of voicing. In the example stimuli, a voicing delay of +10 msec shows the voiced vibration starting almost immediately after the release. A delay of +100 msec shows a long stretch before voicing begins, with noise filling that gap.
The main experiment tested infants in three conditions at each of two age levels. The results for the 4-month-old infants are the data discussed here.
In the first condition, called 20D, the two stimuli differed by 20 msec of VOT and came from different adult phonemic categories. Their VOT values were +20 and +40 msec.
In the second condition, called 20S, the VOT difference was again 20 msec. Now both stimuli came from the same phonemic category. Their values were −20 and 0 msec, or +60 and +80 msec.
The third condition, called 0, was a control condition. These infants heard no change in the sound at all.
The measure was the mean number of sucking responses over time. The letter B marks the baseline rate of sucking before the sounds began. The vertical dashed line marks the moment of the stimulus shift. In the control group, the dashed line marks the time when a shift would have occurred.
Before the shift, all three groups followed the same pattern.
Sucking rose well above baseline while the infants listened to the repeated sound.
Sucking then declined steadily as the same sound continued. This decline reflects the infants getting used to the sound.
After the shift, the groups differed.
In the 20D group, sucking stayed high after the shift. The infants responded as though they had noticed a new sound.
In the 20S group and the 0 control group, sucking kept declining. The 20 msec change within a single category produced no clear recovery, and the control group had no change at all.
The interpretation is that four-month-old infants detect a 20 msec difference only when the two sounds belong to different adult categories. The same 20 msec difference inside one category does not produce a detectable change. This is the same pattern that adult listeners show, and it appears in infants who have no experience with language production.
Section 5: Why Categorical Perception in Infants Was Considered Important

Categorical perception was considered to be the first step in turning speech into a symbolic format that later linguistic processes could use. The continuous, variable sound signal becomes a set of discrete categories. Later stages of language processing can then work with those categories.
Four-month-olds are very young. Their gross motor milestones include manipulating objects with two hands and holding the head and chest up while lying face down. Their color vision and general visual acuity are still developing.
At this age, infants are just starting to produce vowel-like sounds instead of creaky whines. Their own speech production is only beginning.
An apparently language-specific ability that appears this early in development seemed like evidence for innateness. The ability is present before the infant has had much chance to learn from speaking or from the sounds of the environment.
Section 6: Perceptual Narrowing for Speech Categories

The Werker and Tees study (1984) tested how age and experience affect infants’ ability to discriminate non-English contrasts. The two contrasts were a Hindi contrast (ta versus ta, written Ta/ta) and a Salish contrast (ki versus qi, written ki/qi). Neither contrast is used in English.
At around six months, English-learning infants show evidence of discriminating speech contrasts they have never heard before. Most of the infants aged 6 to 8 months reached the discrimination criterion for both contrasts.
Over the course of development, the ability to perceive these contrasts declines.
By 8 to 10 months, only about half to two-thirds of the English-learning infants reached the criterion.
By 10 to 12 months, only a small proportion did.
Infants who are learning Hindi or Salish reach the criterion at nearly every age. The decline therefore belongs to infants who are not exposed to those contrasts. It does not come from a general decline in hearing.
This pattern is called perceptual narrowing. Sensitivity to contrasts that the native language does not use becomes weaker during the first year of life.
Section 7: The Compelling Narrative

The story built from these findings has several connected parts.
Speech perception depends on categorical perception abilities that are a special property of the language system.
These abilities are present very early in development.
Children start off as “citizens of the world.” They can hear all contrasts, which means they have innate, language-general knowledge about speech.
They then rapidly tune their perceptual systems to the specific language in their environment.
On this account, the role of learning is limited. Learning selects among the many possible innately specified phonetic contrasts. It narrows the inventory down to the contrasts that are meaningful in the child’s native language.
This account fits the nativist position from the start of the material. The categories are provided by the child’s biology. Experience decides which of the provided categories stay in use.
Section 8: Some Problems for This View

The account has problems on two fronts. Some concern categorical perception itself, and one concerns infant speech perception.
Categorical perception is not limited to language. Categorical patterns appear for sounds that are not speech. If the pattern appears for non-speech sounds, it cannot be a special property of the language system.
Categorical perception is not limited to humans. Other species show categorical patterns too, including for speech sounds. The early claim that only humans show categorical perception does not hold.
Categorical perception is task-dependent. Whether a categorical pattern appears depends on the task listeners perform. It is therefore not a fixed, automatic property of how sounds are perceived.
Infant speech perception is not actually “universal.” Infants do not start out able to hear every contrast equally well. Some contrasts are harder for infants than others, even before language experience has had any effect.
Section 9: Categorical Perception of Non-Speech Sounds (Cutting and Rosner, 1974)

Cutting and Rosner (1974) tested categorical perception with non-speech sounds. They compared three types of stimuli: sawtooth waves, speech syllables, and sine waves. Each type came in versions with a 10 msec rise time and a 70 msec rise time.
Rise time is how quickly the sound builds up to its full loudness. A short rise time gives a sudden onset. A long rise time gives a gradual onset.
With the sawtooth waves, listeners labeled the sounds as either pluck or bow.
Sounds with short rise times were labeled as plucks.
Sounds with long rise times were labeled as bows.
The labels switched sharply in the middle of the range, much as the labels switch from one consonant to another in speech.
The discrimination results follow the same pattern as the speech results. Listeners were most accurate when the two sounds being compared fell on opposite sides of the pluck and bow boundary. Accuracy was lower when both sounds came from the same side. The sounds are not speech, yet they are perceived categorically.
Section 10: Categorical Perception of Non-Speech Sounds by Infants (Jusczyk et al., 1977)

Jusczyk et al. (1977) tested infants with non-speech sounds using the high amplitude sucking method. The question is whether infants perceive categories in non-speech sounds the way they do for speech.
There were three conditions.
In the no change condition, the infants heard the same sound before and after the shift.
In the within categories condition, the sound changed to a different sound from the same category.
In the between categories condition, the sound changed to a sound from a different category.
The measure was the mean number of sucking responses over time, with baseline marked as B and the stimulus shift marked by the vertical dashed line. In all three groups, sucking declined over the minutes before the shift.
After the shift, the groups differed.
Sucking stayed low in the no change group.
Sucking stayed low in the within categories group.
Sucking recovered in the between categories group. These infants responded as though they noticed a new sound.
Infants therefore perceive categories in non-speech sounds. Recovery depends on whether the sound crossed a category boundary, and the size of the physical change is not the deciding factor. This is the same pattern seen with speech in Eimas et al. (1971). The infant result does not require a mechanism specific to language.

Section 12: Some Contrasts Are Less Salient Than Others for Infants

Narayan et al. (2010) tested a place contrast for nasals in word-initial position. The contrast is between /na/ and /ŋa/, where /ŋ/ is the sound at the end of the English word “sing.” This contrast is not readily perceived by four-month-olds, independent of language experience.
The measure was looking time. Infants heard a series of repeated sounds, and the test compared looking time on same trials with looking time on change trials. If an infant notices the change, looking time on change trials is longer.
English-hearing infants aged four to five months looked longer on change trials for [ma] versus [na]. This is a native contrast, and it was relatively salient.
The same infants showed no clear difference between same and change trials for [na] versus [ŋa]. This is a non-native contrast for English, and the infants did not notice the change.
The infants who failed to notice the change had not yet lost sensitivity through experience. The difficulty belongs to the contrast itself. This is why infant speech perception is not actually universal.
Section 13: Exposure Increases the Salience of the Contrast (Narayan et al., 2010)

The same study tested Filipino-learning infants at 6 to 8 months and at 10 to 12 months. For these infants, the [na] versus [ŋa] contrast is a native contrast, since it is used in their language environment.
The younger group, aged 6 to 8 months, showed no clear difference in looking time between same and change trials. The older group, aged 10 to 12 months, looked longer on change trials. The older infants noticed the change.
Between 6 and 10 months, exposure to this contrast in a Filipino language environment increases its salience. The contrast was hard for four-month-old English-hearing infants. It becomes easier for infants who hear it regularly in their environment.
It therefore cannot be the case that all speech contrasts are available innately. Exposure to contrasts in the environment is necessary for acquisition in some cases. Learning does more than select from a ready-made set. For some contrasts, learning builds the ability to perceive the difference.
Section 14: The Alternative to the Nativist Approach

Emergentist approaches assume that associative learning mechanisms are sensitive to statistical regularities in the environment. The structure of language is not supplied in advance. It emerges from what the learner experiences, through mechanisms that detect patterns in the input.
The classic example of associative learning is conditioning. The pictured circuit has three parts: a bell unit, a food detector unit, and a salivation unit. The connection from the bell to the salivation unit is labeled as the synapse to be strengthened.
Food reliably activates salivation.
When the bell sounds at the same time as the food, the connection between the bell and salivation is strengthened.
After enough pairings, the bell alone can activate salivation.
The emergentist idea extends this kind of learning beyond conditioning to support learning for pattern recognition. The same principle, in which connections are strengthened by regularities in what is experienced, can apply to the patterns found in speech sounds.
Section 15: Learning the Statistics of Speech Sound Distributions

The distribution of American vowels can be plotted in a vowel space that is determined by the first and second formants. Each point is one production of a vowel. The first formant is plotted along the horizontal axis, and the second formant along the vertical axis.
Each vowel forms a cloud of points, and each cloud is surrounded by an outline. The clouds for different vowels overlap with their neighbors. This overlap is why speech categorization is difficult. A single sound can sometimes fall within the range of more than one vowel.
When Kuhl and others talk about learning the statistics of distributions in the environment, they mean patterns like these. The learner hears many tokens of each vowel. The tokens cluster in particular regions of the vowel space.
One possibility is that we learn the vowels of our language by extracting the statistics of these distributions.
Under this idea, a “prototypical” /a/ is the mean (or mode) of the distribution of all the /a/ sounds we hear. The prototype is not stored as an innate template. It is the center of what has been heard.
Section 16: Evidence for Distributional Learning (Maye et al., 2002)

Maye et al. (2002, Cognition) tested whether anything like this actually happens. They had infants listen passively to two distributions of voice onset time along an eight-step continuum from [da] to [ta]. Step 1 is the endpoint [da] stimulus, and step 8 is the endpoint [ta] stimulus.
The two distributions differed in how often each step occurred during familiarization.
The dashed line marks a bimodal distribution. Steps near the two ends of the continuum occurred most often, with a dip in the middle. The input therefore provided evidence for two different categories.
The solid line marks a unimodal distribution. Steps near the middle occurred most often. The input therefore provided evidence for a single category.
At test, infants listened to stimuli 3 and 6 on the continuum. They heard them in either alternating or non-alternating trials. As the names indicate, alternating trials present the two different stimuli in alternation, and non-alternating trials repeat a single stimulus. Infants were tested at 6 months and at 8 months.
Only infants pre-exposed to the bimodal distribution were sensitive to the difference between conditions.
The infants in the bimodal groups showed different looking times for alternating and non-alternating trials, at both ages.
The infants in the unimodal groups looked for about the same amount of time in both kinds of trials. For these infants, the two conditions did not differ.
It is as if the infants in the unimodal condition had learned to treat stimuli 3 and 6 as members of the same category. The bimodal group had learned to treat them as different. The same two test sounds led to different behavior, and the only difference between the groups was the statistical pattern of the sounds they had heard earlier.
Section 17: Social and Interactive Context for Learning

There is experimental evidence that social interaction is important for learning. Two sets of experiments are described.
In the first set, nine-month-old American infants were exposed to Mandarin Chinese in twelve 25-minute sessions. Some infants received live exposure. Others received the same exposure through television or audio only. A control group heard English.
After exposure, infants were tested on a Mandarin phonetic contrast using the head-turn procedure.
The infants in the live exposure group showed phonetic learning. Their percent correct was clearly above that of the English control group.
The infants in the TV and audio-only groups showed no learning. Their performance stayed at the level of the English control group, which was close to chance.
The same exposure to a Mandarin speaker is more effective for learning when it happens in person, and is interactive, than when it is viewed as a video.
In the second set of experiments, eight-month-old infants received contingent or non-contingent social feedback from their mothers in response to their vocalizations.
The contingent condition gave a social response that depended on what the infant produced. The yoked-control condition gave social responses that were not tied to the infant’s own vocalizations.
Each visit had four phases: familiarization (30 min), baseline (10 min), social response (10 min), and extinction (10 min). In the extinction phase, the social responses stopped.
Contingent social feedback increased the quantity and complexity of infants’ vocalizations. The proportion of syllables among the vocalizations rose sharply in the social response phase for the contingent group. It stayed above baseline in the extinction phase. The non-contingent group stayed low throughout.
Social interactions contingent on the production of different sounds seem to function much like a reinforcing stimulus. The infant’s sounds change in response to the feedback, as behavior changes in response to reinforcement.
Two ideas could explain why social context matters.
One idea is that humans find social interaction with other humans innately reinforcing.
The other idea is that we are innately good at using social cues to learn from other people.
Section 18: Statistical Learning with Constraints

Statistical learning is not unlimited. It operates under constraints, and the term covers several things.
The first constraint concerns what stimulus properties can be contrastive. Properties such as speaker and speech rate are not treated as the basis for a contrast between speech categories. A different speaker or a different speaking rate does not create a new category.
The second constraint comes from earlier learning. What a learner has already learned affects what can be learned next. This idea returns in the discussion of sensitive periods.
The third constraint is the social and/or interactive context for learning. As the Mandarin and vocalization experiments indicate, the social setting in which sounds are heard affects whether learning occurs.
