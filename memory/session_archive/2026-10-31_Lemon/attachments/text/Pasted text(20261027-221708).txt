Section 1: What Speech Perception Has to Accomplish

Speech perception is the process of taking the sound waves of someone talking and recovering the message they intended. The physical signal is messy, but listeners still pull a clear message out of it almost instantly. Two pictures of speech make clear how messy the signal is: one of the moving vocal tract, and one of the sound it produces.
The vocal tract picture comes from USC SPAN, which uses real-time MRI to film the inside of a speaker’s head while they talk. Colored outlines trace the lips, tongue, palate, and the back of the throat. The shapes of these structures change constantly during speech, and every change alters the sound coming out of the mouth.
The second picture is a spectrogram of the sentence “I can see you.” A spectrogram plots time on the horizontal axis, from 0 to 1.9 seconds here, and frequency on the vertical axis, from 0 to 7000 cycles per second (Hz). Darker regions mean more energy at that frequency and moment.
The spectrogram of “I can see you” has no clean breaks between words. The voiced parts appear as dark horizontal bands that bend up and down as the mouth moves. There is a blank gap near 0.4 to 0.55 seconds, where the airflow stops for the “c” in “can.” The “s” in “see,” near 1.0 to 1.2 seconds, appears as a dense block of noise at high frequencies, roughly 4000 to 7000 Hz. None of these patterns looks like separate letters or words, yet listeners hear a sentence.
Listeners also extract the same message from very different physical stimuli. Men, women, children, older adults, and people with different accents all produce different sound waves when they say the same word. A listener still recognizes the word across all of these speakers.
A plot of vowel measurements makes this concrete. It graphs the frequency of the second formant against the frequency of the first formant for ten vowels produced by 76 speakers. Each vowel forms a cloud of points circled by an ellipse, and the ellipses are large and overlap with one another. The same vowel spoken by different people lands in different places, and different vowels sometimes land in the same place.
Section 2: The Premise That Speech Must Be Translated Into Symbols

One premise about speech perception is that the brain must turn a highly variable, analog signal into something more abstract. An analog signal changes smoothly and continuously, like the bands in the “I can see you” spectrogram. The language system, which handles words and grammar, is thought to work on discrete units such as phonemes. So the continuous sound has to be converted into a small set of separate categories before the rest of the language system can operate on it.
Categorical perception is thought to reflect this kind of “translation” from physical to symbolic. In categorical perception, a listener hears a continuous range of sounds as belonging to a few distinct categories rather than as a smooth series of tiny changes. If perception sorts sounds into bins like /b/, /d/, and /g/, then the output of perception is already symbolic, and the language system can use it directly.
This view is one account of how the translation happens. Other accounts of the same problem exist and are the subject of the next topic in the course.
Section 3: Formants and the Shape of the Vocal Tract

The vocal tract is a tube that runs from the vocal folds to the lips. When air vibrates inside this tube, some frequencies are strengthened more than others. These strengthened frequency regions are called formants. The shape of the vocal tract is related in lawful ways to the amplification of particular resonant frequencies, a relationship described by Ladefoged (1996).
“Lawful” means the link is predictable. If you know where the tongue, jaw, and lips are, you can predict roughly where the formants will fall. If the tongue moves, the formants move in a consistent direction.
Ladefoged’s figure pairs a drawing of the vocal tract with a frequency curve for eight English words: heed, hid, head, had, hod, hawed, hood, and who’d. Each curve plots frequency from 0 to about 3000 Hz, and each peak in the curve is a formant.
For heed, the tongue is high and pushed toward the front of the mouth. The first peak sits very low, near the bottom of the scale, and the next peaks sit high, above 2000 Hz. This gives a large gap between the first and second formant.
Moving through hid, head, and had, the tongue and jaw drop step by step. The first peak moves up in frequency, and the second peak moves down. By had, the three peaks sit at roughly 700, 1700, and 2500 Hz, spread fairly evenly across the scale.
For hod and hawed, the tongue is pulled back and low. The first two peaks crowd together below about 1200 Hz, and little energy is left at higher frequencies.
For hood and who’d, the tongue is high and pulled back, and the lips are rounded. The first and second peaks are both very low, under about 1000 Hz, and they shrink in height. Who’d has the lowest pair of peaks of all eight words.
The pattern across these words is that tongue height mainly tracks the first formant and tongue frontness mainly tracks the second formant. A lower tongue and more open jaw raise the first formant. A tongue farther forward raises the second formant. This is the lawful relationship that lets listeners recover vowel identity from formant frequencies.
