Original slide text
A stable message in a variable signal
Speech perception allows a listener to recognize words in a changing sound signal. Somehow, the sound of “I can see you” becomes a message with recognizable words, even though the recording contains no separate acoustic pieces corresponding to the written letters. The movements used to produce neighboring sounds overlap, so their acoustic effects overlap as well.

A speaker’s voice also changes the physical form of an utterance. Different speakers can produce the same vowel with different frequencies, and some frequencies can overlap across vowels. Matching a recording to one fixed sound pattern would therefore leave many ordinary productions unrecognized.

Recognizing speech requires finding a linguistic identity within this physical variation. The words can remain the same when the voice changes, because the message depends on how the sounds function in the language rather than on an exact repetition of every acoustic detail.

Original slide text
From an analog signal to a symbolic representation Must know · core review
Somehow we have to turn a highly variable, analog signal into something more abstract, so the language system can operate on it. An analog signal changes continuously. Frequency, loudness, and timing can each vary by small amounts, but every small change does not create a different speech sound.

A symbolic representation identifies a language unit rather than preserving every physical detail of the recording. A phoneme is a sound category that helps distinguish words. Different acoustic productions can count as the same phoneme, allowing different recordings to supply the same linguistic unit.

Categorical perception is one proposed way of making this translation. Listeners become less sensitive to some differences within a category and more sensitive to similar differences that cross its boundary. A change in the sound is especially noticeable when it changes the identity assigned to it. This organization can preserve useful distinctions while allowing variation within one identity.

Formants reflect the shape of the vocal tract Must know · core review
The vocal tract is the passage through which sound travels during speech. Its shape changes as the tongue, lips, and mouth move. Those changes affect which frequencies receive the strongest amplification, so the sound reaching the listener depends partly on the shape of this passage.

Formants are the resonant frequencies emphasized by the vocal tract. Vowels such as those in heed, hid, head, and had differ in their combinations of formants. The remaining examples, including hod, hawed, hood, and who’d, involve other combinations. A vowel is characterized by the relationship among resonances rather than by one frequency that stays identical across speakers.

A formant transition is a change in one of these resonances over time. The transition occurs because the vocal tract is moving into or out of a sound. A steady portion supplies information about a vowel, while the movement toward that portion also carries information about how the sound is produced.

Original slide text
The pattern playback synthesizer
Stop consonants and formant transitions
The stop consonants /b/, /d/, and /g/ differ partly in their formant transitions. These transitions are movements in resonance as the vocal tract opens into the following vowel. The audible part of a stop is produced during movement, so consonant information is carried by how the frequencies change rather than only by a stable portion of sound.

Liberman and colleagues (1957) created synthetic syllables with a similar lower formant and different starting frequencies for the upper formant. The upper transition could rise, remain relatively flat, or fall before reaching a similar vowel region. The following vowel could remain alike while its onset changed.

The pattern playback synthesizer allowed these properties to be controlled. Listeners heard speech-like sounds whose transitions differed in a specified way. Their responses could then be related to the transition itself, rather than to an uncontrolled collection of differences between natural utterances.

Why reduced sensitivity can help recognition Must know · core review
Linking categorical perception to word knowledge
Measuring infant speech discrimination
The conditioned head turn procedure measures whether an infant detects a change in sound. The infant learns an association between the change and a visual reward. Turning toward the sound provides an observable response without requiring the infant to name what was heard.

High-amplitude sucking measures a different behavior. Sucking can decline as a repeated sound becomes familiar. If a new sound causes sucking to increase again, the change has affected the infant’s behavior and provides evidence that the sounds were distinguished.

Both methods make perception testable before spoken responses are available. The experimenter controls the sound difference and measures a learned turn or renewed sucking. These responses establish sensitivity to the change, rather than requiring an infant to perform an adult labeling task.

Eimas · categorical discrimination before words Must know · core review
Perceptual narrowing across infancy Must know · core review
Perceptual narrowing occurs when experience tunes perception to the surrounding language while sensitivity to some other distinctions decreases. English-learning infants initially distinguish the tested Hindi and Salish contrasts relatively well. Their performance becomes poorer as the first year progresses.

Many infants meet the discrimination criterion at 6–8 months. Fewer do so at 8–10 months, and performance is much lower at 10–12 months. Infants learning Hindi or Salish retain stronger discrimination for the contrast used in their language.

Werker and Tees relate this developmental change to experience with a particular sound system. The infant becomes adapted to distinctions that occur in that system. The same development can leave distinctions from other languages harder to detect, rather than preserving equally strong sensitivity to every speech contrast.

The initially broad perception account
Separating early appearance from language specificity
Cutting and Rosner · categories for nonspeech Must know · core review
Cutting and Rosner (1974) tested categorical perception with nonspeech sounds that differed in rise time. Rise time is how quickly a sound becomes stronger after it begins. A rapid onset sounds plucked, while a slower onset sounds bowed.

The materials included sawtooth and sine waves, with speech syllables included for comparison. Listeners could assign pluck or bow labels even though the manipulated sounds were not different phonemes. The relevant property was the rate of increase in amplitude.

For sawtooth waves, increasing rise time changed the preferred label from pluck to bow. Discrimination was strongest near that labeling change rather than remaining constant for equally spaced sounds. The relationship between a category boundary and improved discrimination can therefore occur with a nonlinguistic auditory distinction.

Pluck and bow are perceptual labels for different onset qualities, not names of phonemes. The physical continuum remains gradual across the change in preferred label. Improved discrimination near that change links the result to categorical organization without requiring the listener to interpret the sounds as speech.

Original slide text
Jusczyk · infant categories for nonspeech
Jusczyk and colleagues (1977) tested infants’ responses to nonspeech sounds. After a sound was repeated, the experiment compared no change, a change within a category, and a change between categories. Sucking behavior provided the measure of whether the change was detected.

Sucking declined as the repeated sound became familiar in all conditions. It stayed relatively low after no change and after a within-category change. A between-category change caused sucking to increase again, followed by another decline as that sound became familiar.

The boundary-crossing change affected behavior more strongly than a change within the category. Infants can therefore display category-related discrimination for sounds that are not speech. An ability’s early appearance does not establish that it operates only on phonemes or only within language.

Original slide text
Chinchillas categorize speech continua Must know · core review
Chinchillas learned to respond to speech sounds through operant conditioning, in which consequences change the likelihood of a behavior. A stimulus with 0-millisecond or 80-millisecond lag signaled that a response would produce food. This rewarded signal is called the SD, or discriminative stimulus.

Tests with intermediate voice onset times measured how the response extended across a /ga/–/ka/ continuum. Chinchillas and human comparison listeners changed their responses sharply within part of that continuum. The animals could categorize the acoustic distinction without possessing human language.

Ohlemiller and colleagues (1999) and Kuhl and Miller (1975) are associated with these comparisons. Making the initial burst and aspiration noise stronger could shift the boundary toward shorter VOTs or change how sharply responses switched. The categorization depended on acoustic properties in both the animal and human responses.

Original slide text
Some infant contrasts must become salient Must know · core review
Some speech distinctions are less salient, meaning harder to notice, than others early in development. At 4–5 months, English-learning infants respond more clearly to [ma]–[na] than to the nonnative [na]–[ŋa] contrast. Increased looking after a change provides evidence that the infant detected it.

These contrasts involve different places of articulation. The nasal in [ma] is made with the lips, the nasal in [na] near the ridge behind the teeth, and the nasal in [ŋa] farther back in the mouth. The comparison concerns whether changing that place changes the infant’s response.

Filipino-learning infants respond weakly to their native [na]–[ŋa] contrast at 6–8 months, but more clearly at 10–12 months. Experience can therefore strengthen a distinction that begins difficult to hear. Speech development includes increased sensitivity to some contrasts as well as declining sensitivity to others.

A staged speech-perception model
The beach–peach voicing continuum
Thresholded transmission Must know · core review
A threshold model requires activity to reach a particular amount before it can affect the next level. Evidence below that threshold can fluctuate without producing a response at the higher level. The higher stage waits until the lower stage has enough support to deliver its selected output.

Feature evidence varies between +voiced and −voiced. Early fluctuations remain below the relevant thresholds, so the phoneme level receives no completed decision. When +voiced activity crosses its threshold, support for /b/ appears abruptly at that level.

Under this account, word recognition receives the selected phoneme rather than the entire history of uncertainty. Two acoustically different inputs that both produce /b/ can therefore become identical for the word stage. The transformation preserves the category but discards differences that existed before the threshold was crossed.

McMurray · the visual-world method Must know · core review
McMurray, Tanenhaus, Aslin, and Spivey (2003) tested spoken-word recognition using a visual-world eye-tracking task. Participants listened to sounds varying between /b/ and /p/ while viewing pictures. The sound manipulation changed the evidence for the initial consonant without replacing the whole word on every trial.

The pictures included bear and pear, which differ in that initial sound, along with unrelated objects such as a lamp and a ship. Participants chose the picture matching what they heard. Their eye movements were measured while recognition was still developing.

A participant could eventually select bear after briefly looking at pear. Those competitor looks provided evidence that the alternative had received some support. The method therefore measured competition preceding the final click, including activity for a word that was ultimately rejected.

Andruski · partial input can activate meaning Must know · core review
Andruski and colleagues (1994) tested whether an imperfect sound match can activate a word’s meaning. Semantic priming occurs when one item makes a meaning-related target easier to process. A priming benefit after an altered spoken form indicates that some information has reached the corresponding word and its meaning.

The nonword beace facilitated responding to war through partial activation of peace. Similarly, ging facilitated queen through king. The input did not have to be accepted as a correctly pronounced word for its partly matching lexical candidate to affect a related meaning.

Related word primes, including bill before drug, were compared with unrelated primes. Altering an initial sound could weaken the benefit rather than eliminating all access. The amount of sound overlap influenced the strength of lexical and semantic activation, instead of acting only as an all-or-none gate.

Beace resembles peace closely enough to provide partial support, even though its beginning is altered. War is related to the meaning of peace rather than to the altered input as a valid dictionary word. The priming relationship therefore reaches beyond phonetic similarity to the activated word’s semantic associations.

Why the threshold-only account struggles
Adding lexical feedback Must know · core review
Ganong · real words bias phoneme labels Must know · core review
Ganong (1980) tested whether word knowledge changes phoneme identification. Participants heard a continuum whose endpoints formed a real word and a nonword. In corpse–gorpse, one interpretation of the initial consonant produces a familiar word, while the other does not.

The onset changed in 5-millisecond steps, varying the acoustic evidence near the consonant boundary. The remaining sounds supplied information about whether each possible beginning would complete a word. The manipulation therefore combined uncertain phonetic evidence with a difference in lexical status.

If lexical knowledge affects identification, an ambiguous consonant should be more likely to receive the interpretation forming the real word. The question concerns the uncertain part of the input. Recognizing an unmistakable word endpoint would not establish the same influence on phoneme categorization.

The cohort effect in spoken-word recognition Must know · core review
In Allopenna and colleagues’ (1994) task, listeners hear a word while viewing possible referents. For beaker, beetle shares the beginning and is called a cohort competitor. Speaker shares the ending and is a rhyme competitor, while carriage is unrelated.

The initial sounds support both beaker and beetle, so looks toward both increase early. Once the sounds distinguishing the words arrive, target looking continues to rise while beetle looking declines. The competitor loses support because the later input no longer matches.

Speaker overlaps at a later point, so its competition has a different time course. Eye movements record when each candidate attracts attention before the final choice. Correctly selecting beaker does not mean that beaker was the only word considered throughout recognition.

Cohort and rhyme overlap are not interchangeable. Beetle is supported while only the beginning has been heard, whereas speaker has a mismatching beginning and gains overlap later. The time at which evidence becomes available determines when each candidate can plausibly compete.

Original slide text
The horse raced past the barn fell Must know · core review
“The horse raced past the barn fell” is grammatical, but the beginning encourages an interpretation that does not fit the ending. A reader can initially take raced as the horse’s main action. That makes the words before fell seem to describe a complete event, leaving the final verb difficult to incorporate.

The intended meaning is “The horse that was raced past the barn fell.” The phrase raced past the barn identifies the horse. Fell then states what happened to it. Omitting “that was” leaves this modifying relationship less explicit.

Sentence comprehension depends on assigning relationships among words, not only recognizing their individual meanings. Here, one action belongs inside the description of the horse and the other is the main event. Difficulty arises when the early interpretation assigns those roles incorrectly.

The horse is the one that fell, not a second participant introduced by the last word. Raced belongs within the description of that horse. Keeping those relationships distinct supplies a complete interpretation without deleting either verb or treating the original sentence as an ungrammatical word list.

An autonomous structural parser Must know · core review
Reduced relatives are not always difficult
A reduced relative clause modifies a noun while omitting words such as “that was.” In “Recent research reported by Ferreira and Clifton,” reported describes research that someone reported. Research is not itself performing the reporting as the main action of the sentence.

“Referential information provided by the discourse,” “results obtained with global reading times,” and “examples cited here” have the same kind of modifier. Provided, obtained, and cited relate to their preceding nouns. These forms can be understood readily despite the complexity associated with reduced relatives.

MacDonald, Perlmutter, and Seidenberg (1994) argue that meaning and learned usage patterns contribute early to parsing. The formal structure alone does not determine difficulty. Particular words and their familiar relationships can support a reduced-relative interpretation without producing a strong garden path.

Why the example table matters
Research reported and the arrested burglar Must know · core review
Spivey · an apple, a towel, and a box Must know · core review
Spivey and colleagues (1995) asked people to follow spoken instructions while viewing objects. The one-apple display contained a pencil, a box, an empty towel, and an apple on a towel. Participants heard “Put the apple on the towel in the box.”

The phrase on the towel can initially sound like a destination, prompting a plan to put the apple on the empty towel. In the intended interpretation, it identifies where the apple already is. In the box supplies the actual destination.

Participants often looked toward the empty towel before turning toward the box. With “Put the apple that’s on the towel in the box,” the modifier is explicit and this temporary error is reduced. Eye movements reveal a competing interpretation considered before the final action is completed.

The two towels play different roles. One already supports the apple, while the empty towel is the competing destination. Confusing the current-location modifier with the destination changes the planned action, even though the listener recognizes all the object names correctly.

The early left anterior negativity Must know · core review
The early left anterior negativity, or ELAN, is an event-related potential associated with word-class violations. An event-related potential is a measured brain response aligned to an event such as a word’s onset. This response occurs roughly 100–200 milliseconds after the critical word, with a left/anterior distribution.

In “The pizza was in the eaten,” a participle appears where the intended phrase expects a noun. In “The scientist criticized Max’s of proof the theorem,” the preposition of appears where it does not fit the developing noun phrase. Both involve a mismatch in grammatical category.

The early response motivated an account involving rapid syntactic analysis. The critical distinction is between a word of an unexpected grammatical type and a correctly positioned word with an implausible meaning. The measurement concerns a response very soon after the relevant form becomes available.

When a category violation produces the early effect Must know · core review
The early ELAN response depends partly on how much a word’s form reveals about its category. It occurs for nouns with informative morphological marking, meaning meaningful word parts, and for forms strongly associated with nouns. A neutral form such as infant does not produce the same early pattern.

The comparisons include beautiful princess versus beautifully princess, tasteless soda versus tastelessly soda, and cute infant versus cutely infant. Princess provides informative morphology, soda has a statistically noun-like form, and infant supplies weaker form-based category evidence.

The abstract category mismatch is therefore not sufficient to determine this earliest visual response. An expected grammatical category can generate predictions about word form. The response depends partly on whether the word that arrives contains physical evidence conflicting with that prediction.

Four sentences, four connected lessons Must know · core review
“Colorless green ideas sleep furiously” has an acceptable grammatical organization but an implausible combined meaning. Syntax and semantics are partly independent because words can fit a structure without forming a sensible event. Grammatical organization is not simply the same property as plausibility.

“The horse raced past the barn fell” becomes difficult when an early structural preference cannot accommodate the final verb. Such preferences can reduce memory demands while leaving some sentences vulnerable to revision. In the apple instruction, however, the available objects affect early interpretation of the ambiguous phrase.

Word forms also carry learned category information. “The bird perches were comfortable” uses a noun-like form as a noun, while other continuations require a different use. Early category-related brain responses depend partly on such form-based expectations. Parsing therefore involves grammatical relationships together with meaning, context, and learned regularities in the available input.

Centers, connections, and disconnections
The Geschwind–Wernicke framework
Fluency, agrammatism, and comprehension
Telegraphic speech versus fluent word salad Must know · core review
Wernicke’s aphasia can involve fluent “word salad” or “Jabberwocky.” A patient describing a visual problem said “My wires don’t hire right.” The expression has a sentence-like arrangement, but the selected words fail to communicate the intended problem with vision.

Other responses continue with grammatical-sounding phrases that do not form a clear message. “I felt worse because I can no longer keep in mind from the mind of the minds…” retains fluency without a coherent explanation. Invented forms such as “demaploze” and “tubaboys” are neologisms. Calling a fork “a need for a schedule” similarly fails to identify the object despite producing familiar words.

Broca’s aphasia can instead produce telegraphic speech. A hospital account retains words about Dad, the hospital, doctors, appointments, and teeth, but pauses and missing grammatical connections make the event hard to express. The surviving content words convey parts of the experience even though sentence production is restricted.

Dick · four ways to describe the same action Must know · core review
In Dick and colleagues’ task, listeners identify which animal performs the action. The event is a dog biting a cat, but the sentence structure changes. Knowing the animal names is insufficient because the answer depends on assigning their roles.

Saygin · actions without words Must know · core review
Saygin and colleagues (2004) asked participants to match actions with objects. A nonverbal version conveyed an action such as licking without spoken language and required choosing a fitting object, such as an ice cream. A linguistic version supplied language-based information for the same general decision.

Aphasic participants were less accurate than controls in both versions. The difficulty was therefore not confined to understanding grammatical sentences. The nonverbal task still required identifying the relevant action meaning and connecting it to an appropriate object.

Semantically related distractors caused more difficulty than unrelated choices. An object related in meaning competed more strongly with the correct match. Selecting the intended meaning could therefore be disrupted even when no linguistic sentence had to be interpreted.

The nonverbal comparison removes a linguistic expression without removing meaning from the task. The patient still has to identify what the action calls for and select the corresponding object. Similar difficulty in that condition therefore concerns a demand shared with language rather than grammar alone.

Voxel-based lesion–symptom mapping Must know · core review
Voxel-based lesion–symptom mapping relates the location of brain damage to behavioral impairment across patients. A voxel is a small three-dimensional unit of an MRI image. Comparing damaged and spared locations against symptom measures identifies regions associated with reduced performance.

Fluency deficits have stronger associations with anterior, or front, regions. Comprehension deficits have stronger associations with posterior, or back, regions. These measures concern broad speaking and understanding abilities rather than direct tests of one grammatical operation.

The distributions support a rough double dissociation between production-related and comprehension-related difficulties. Different patterns of damage have different behavioral associations. Mapping across patients provides a quantitative relationship between tissue location and symptoms rather than relying only on a single case description.

Nativist explanations of language acquisition Must know · core review
The nature-and-nurture question concerns how built-in capacities and experience contribute to language acquisition. Nativist positions developed from Chomsky’s critique of Skinner. Skinner relied on general learning mechanisms such as reward and punishment, while Chomsky argued that these mechanisms could not explain the grammar children acquire.

For Chomsky, language’s important property is generativity. People understand and produce new sentences they have never heard. Acquiring a language therefore means identifying the abstract grammar that produces its sentences, rather than memorizing a collection of earlier utterances.

Grammatical and ungrammatical sentences are not similar in any straightforward way. A small change can make a sentence unacceptable, while two acceptable sentences can look very different. Grouping expressions by surface resemblance cannot simply separate everything permitted by the grammar from everything excluded.

Operant conditioning changes behavior through rewards and punishments. Children are not consistently rewarded for grammatical sentences and punished for ungrammatical ones, so those required contingencies do not reliably occur. Nativist accounts instead propose Universal Grammar and language-specific acquisition processes. Innate constraints limit the grammars considered, while a specially equipped “data-handling” or “hypothesis-formulating” ability helps identify the surrounding language.

An abstract grammar captures relationships that extend beyond the particular sentences a child has heard. Generativity creates the learning problem because memorized utterances cannot supply every novel sentence. The proposed innate constraints concern the kinds of grammar considered, while experience still determines which language the child acquires.

Narayan and colleagues (2010) compared infants’ responses to nasal place contrasts. /ma/–/na/ changes from a nasal made with the lips to one made near the ridge behind the teeth. /na/–/ŋa/ changes from that location to one farther back in the mouth.

Learning through social interaction Must know · core review
Social interaction affects language learning from exposure. Nine-month-old American infants received twelve 25-minute sessions before a head-turn test of a Mandarin contrast. Live Mandarin interaction improved performance compared with English exposure. Television and audio-only Mandarin did not produce the same benefit.

The interactive setting changed the effect of hearing another language. Exposure through a recording was not equivalent to exchanging behavior with a live speaker. The result connects learning to how the acoustic input is encountered, not only to whether it is available.

Maternal responses also differed in whether they were contingent, timed to an infant’s vocalization, or noncontingent, delivered at unrelated times. Contingent feedback more strongly increased syllable-like vocalizations. Proposed explanations include an early attraction to social interaction and the ability to use another person’s cues. The timing of a response can affect the learner’s own vocal behavior.

Contingent and noncontingent responses differ in their relationship to the infant’s behavior. A contingent response follows the vocalization at the relevant time, making the exchange responsive. The contrast tests that relationship rather than merely whether an adult is present or whether the infant hears speech at all.

Why speech does not supply printed spaces Must know · core review
Word segmentation is the process of finding words in continuous speech. Spoken language does not contain a reliable equivalent of written spaces. Listeners recover recurring forms from a “sea of sounds” even when the acoustic signal does not clearly mark each beginning and ending.

Silence is not a dependable word boundary. A brief closure of the vocal tract can create silence inside a word during consonant production. Treating every silent interval as a boundary would therefore divide some words incorrectly.

Segmentation requires using evidence about how sounds group together. Identifying a recurring sound unit is also different from knowing its meaning. A learner can begin detecting a word-shaped sequence before establishing what object, action, or property it names.

Stress and phonotactic cues to boundaries Must know · core review
Some words are heard in isolation, including “Hello,” “No,” “Please,” “Go,” and “Stop.” Their boundaries are relatively accessible because the whole utterance contains one word. Many words occur mainly within longer utterances, so isolated examples cannot explain all segmentation.

Phonotactics concerns which sound combinations are common or permitted in a language. Unusual combinations in wordboundary or speechcode can support a boundary, although movement cautions against treating every apparent join as two whole words. These cues concern learned sound patterns rather than visible spaces.

Stress supplies another cue because many English words begin with a strong syllable. Jusczyk and colleagues (1999) familiarized 7.5-month-olds with kingdom or device. Infants preferred passages containing the initially stressed kingdom but not the same way for device, stressed on its second syllable. Detecting a familiar form in running speech depended on how stress aligned with its boundary.

Stress is not a universal solution to segmentation. Different languages organize stress differently, and not every English word begins with a strong syllable. The kingdom–device contrast identifies how a useful regularity can favor one word pattern while leaving another harder to recognize in connected speech.

Joint attention and reference Must know · core review
Joint attention occurs when two people share a focus on an object or event. An adult and child coordinate what the interaction concerns. This provides information about reference beyond whatever happens to attract the child’s attention independently.

Gaze following develops around nine months. Following the adult’s eyes can reveal which object the adult is attending to. When a label is spoken, that focus provides evidence about the object it may name.

Shared attention narrows the possible referents of the word. The child can relate the expression to the adult’s focus rather than associating it equally with everything nearby. The useful information concerns a relationship among the speaker, the learner, and the object.
