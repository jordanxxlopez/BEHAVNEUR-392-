Section 1: A Very Old and Very General Model of Speech Perception

Categorical perception is where this topic starts. Somewhere between the sound entering the ear and the brain deciding which phonemes it heard, a lot of acoustic detail is thrown away. The signal that comes through the cochlea is analog, which means it changes smoothly and continuously. By the time we reach phoneme activation, also called segmental analysis, that smooth signal has become a small set of discrete categories, such as /b/ or /p/.
Klatt (1979) offers a very old and very general model of how this happens. It treats speech perception as a series of stages. Each stage takes in one kind of information, transforms it, and passes a new kind of information to the next stage.
The speech waveform first enters the peripheral auditory system, which is the ear and the earliest parts of hearing. This system performs a spectral analysis, breaking the sound into its different frequencies.
The spectral analysis is passed to acoustic property detectors. These detectors respond to specific acoustic properties, and their outputs are tracked as they change over time.
The detector outputs then go to phonetic feature detectors. These detectors estimate how likely it is that particular phonetic features are present at each moment, such as whether a sound is voiced. The result is a set of feature probabilities over time.
Those feature probabilities are passed to segmental analysis. This stage combines the features into individual speech segments and produces a segmental feature matrix, which is essentially a list of the phonemes that were heard.
Finally, the segmental feature matrix is used in lexical search, where the system looks for words in memory that match the phoneme string. The output is a set of lexical hypotheses, which are the system’s best guesses about which word was spoken.
In this view, losing information is helpful. Removing fine acoustic detail lets the system make contact with higher levels of representation, such as words. A word’s entry in memory is stored as a sequence of phonemes, not as a precise acoustic recording. So reducing the signal to phoneme categories makes it easier to match with stored words.
One assumption follows from this. In order to identify words, we first need to identify their constituent phonemes. Under this assumption, word recognition can only begin after phoneme identification has finished.
This simple view runs into two problems:
Cascaded processing
Feedback
Each of these challenges the idea that information moves one way, one finished stage at a time, from sound to phonemes to words.
Section 2: A Familiar Voicing Continuum

A voicing continuum is a series of speech sounds that change step by step along one acoustic dimension. Here the endpoints are the words “peach” and “beach.” The dimension that changes is voice onset time (VOT).
Voice onset time is the delay between releasing a consonant, such as opening the lips for /p/ or /b/, and the start of vocal fold vibration. A long VOT is heard as the voiceless /p/ in “peach.” As VOT gets shorter, the sound moves toward the voiced /b/ in “beach.”
The continuum has nine steps. Step 1 is at the “peach” end, and step 9 is at the “beach” end. Each step shortens VOT by the same small amount.
In the identification task, listeners hear one step at a time and label it. The labels change suddenly rather than gradually.
Steps 1 through 5 are given the same label every time, at a proportion of 1.
Steps 7 through 9 are given the other label every time, at a proportion of 0.
Step 6 is the ambiguous one. It gets the first label only about a third of the time. This step sits near the category boundary, where listeners switch from one category to the other.
The physical change between steps is the same everywhere on the continuum. However, the labels stay flat across most steps and then flip quickly in one narrow region.
In the discrimination task, listeners hear pairs of stimuli two steps apart and decide whether they are different.
Pairs within the same category, such as steps 1 and 3 or steps 7 and 9, are almost never told apart. Their discrimination score is 0.
Pairs that straddle the boundary, steps 4 and 6 and steps 5 and 7, are told apart perfectly.
The pair of steps 6 and 8 falls in between, at about a third.
So discrimination is strongest exactly where identification changes. This match between the two tasks is the signature of categorical perception. Listeners can easily hear a difference when two sounds fall into different categories, but they struggle when the sounds belong to the same category.
Section 3: How Categorical Perception Happens in a Threshold Model

A threshold model explains categorical perception in one possible way. In this kind of model, activity is passed forward from one level to the next only when an activity threshold is crossed. A threshold is a fixed cutoff value. Below the cutoff, nothing is sent onward. Above it, a signal is sent.
At the feature level, voicing information can rise and fall over time. In the model’s example, feature activation moves up and down between two dashed lines. The upper line is the threshold for +voiced, and the lower line is the threshold for –voiced.
For most of the time, the voicing activation wavers in the middle. It comes close to the +voiced line but does not cross it.
Only near the end does the activation finally rise above the +voiced threshold.
At the phoneme level, activation stays completely flat at zero while the feature level wavers. The phoneme level receives nothing during this time.
When the voicing feature crosses the +voiced threshold, phoneme activation suddenly jumps up to /b/.
If the feature had crossed the lower threshold instead, the phoneme level would have jumped to /p/.
The phoneme level never “sees” the near misses at the feature level. Partial evidence is invisible to it.
In a threshold model, categorical perception arises at phoneme identification. The all-or-none jump in identification happens at the point where the phoneme level decides /b/ or /p/. Any uncertainty below that point is removed before the information moves upward.
Section 4: How Categorical Perception Happens in a Cascaded Model

A cascaded model works differently. In this kind of model, activity is passed forward continuously over time from one level to the next. No cutoff has to be crossed first.
With the same pattern of feature activation, the phoneme level now behaves very differently.
As the voicing feature rises and falls, phoneme activation rises and falls along with it.
When the feature leans toward +voiced, /b/ becomes partly active. When it leans toward –voiced, /p/ becomes partly active.
So the phoneme level carries a graded, moment-by-moment version of the evidence, including partial and uncertain states.
In a cascaded model, categorical perception might happen later on. The sharp category decision might be made at a higher stage instead of at the phoneme level.
It is even possible that categorical perception is an artifact of the task structure. Identification tasks force listeners to give one of two labels. That forced choice could create the sudden switch in the data, even if the underlying activation is graded.
Section 5: A Metaphor for Thresholded Activity: Making Furniture

Thresholded processing can be compared to the steps in making furniture. Each step is handled by a different place that takes one kind of input and produces one kind of output.
The lumber mill takes trees as input and produces lumber as output.
The furniture factory takes lumber as input and produces unassembled furniture as output.
The IKEA customer takes unassembled furniture as input and produces finished furniture as output.
In this chain, each “layer” of the system can only take a specific input that has been transformed completely by the previous layer.
The furniture factory cannot work with a half-cut tree. It has to wait for the shipment of lumber before it can start manufacturing.
In the same way, the IKEA customer cannot begin assembly until the factory has finished making the parts and shipped them.
Mapped onto speech, the word level would have to wait until the phoneme level had fully finished deciding which phonemes were heard.
Many computer programs function this way. One routine finishes its job completely, and only then is its output handed to the next routine.
Section 6: A Metaphor for Cascaded Processing: A Food Web

Cascaded processing can be compared to a food web, such as one in a desert. In a food web, the “levels” are the different groups of living things. Desert plants are eaten by insects, small lizards, and kangaroo rats. Those animals are eaten by larger animals, such as large lizards, scorpions, and snakes. At the top are predators such as hawks and desert foxes.
In a food web, the levels continuously interact with one another. A change at one level spreads to other levels right away, without waiting for anything to be “finished.”
Suppose a drought reduces the number of plants. That reduction immediately affects the health of the kangaroo rats that feed on the plants.
Weaker or fewer kangaroo rats then affect the snakes that eat them, and the effects continue upward.
No level waits for a completed product from the level below. Each one responds to gradual changes as they happen.
The food web also contains clear feedback loops. Effects do not only travel upward from plants to predators. They can also travel back down.
For example, adding more hawks would change the populations of the snakes, lizards, and rats that hawks eat. A change at the top of the web pushes back down onto the lower levels.
This idea of influence flowing downward becomes important later, when word knowledge appears to affect phoneme perception.
Section 7: Thresholded or Cascaded? The McMurray et al. Visual World Study

The main question is whether phoneme identification for word recognition is more like thresholded processing, like the lumber mill, or cascaded processing, like the food web.
McMurray, Tanenhaus, Aslin, and Spivey (2003) tested this question. Their paper is titled “Probabilistic constraint satisfaction at the lexical/phonetic interface: Evidence for gradient effects of within-category VOT on lexical access,” published in the Journal of Psycholinguistic Research.
They tested how people identify sounds along a /b/–/p/ continuum.
They used a “visual world” display combined with eye-tracking. In a visual world task, people see a set of pictures on a screen while they hear speech. An eye tracker records where their eyes look, moment by moment.
Each display contained four pictures.
One was the target, such as a pear.
One was a competitor that differed from the target only in voicing, such as a bear. “Bear” and “pear” form a minimal pair, which means they differ by only one sound.
Two were filler items unrelated to the sounds, such as a lamp and a ship.
Participants heard a word from the continuum and used the mouse to select the picture most consistent with what they heard, either the bear or the pear. This is the same kind of picture-choice identification task as the at-home experiment.
The mouse clicks gave a typical, step-like identification function. At short VOTs, near 0 ms, listeners almost never chose the /p/ picture. At long VOTs, near 40 ms, they almost always did.
The switch happened sharply around the category boundary, which was at about 17.5 ms of VOT, give or take 0.83 ms.
So the final clicks looked just as categorical as the classic identification data from the beach–peach continuum.
Section 8: Eye Movements Reveal That Ambiguity Is Gradient

The mouse clicks were categorical, but the eye movements told a different story. Eye movements revealed that ambiguity is gradient. A gradient effect changes smoothly and gradually rather than all at once. Specifically, people made more looks to the competitor when the sound was close to the category boundary.
The researchers measured how often people looked at the competitor picture over time after the word began. They did this separately for trials where the final click went to the bear and trials where it went to the pear.
On trials where people clicked the bear, the VOTs ranged from 0 to 15 ms. Looks to the pear rose and peaked roughly 400 ms after the word began, then faded away.
The 15 ms stimulus, which is the one closest to the boundary on the bear side, produced the most looks to the pear. It also kept those looks going for longer.
On trials where people clicked the pear, the VOTs ranged from 20 to 40 ms. Here the 20 ms stimulus, closest to the boundary on the pear side, drew the most looks to the bear.
This result is important. Even when the listener’s final answer was the same, the eye movements were sensitive to small differences in VOT within a category.
A 0 ms token and a 15 ms token were both clicked as “bear.” Yet the 15 ms token pulled the eyes toward “pear” much more.
According to the authors, the effect of VOT is mainly on the duration of activation. Stimuli near the boundary kept the competitor active for longer before it faded.
The effect becomes even clearer when the looks to the competitor are averaged over two time windows. The early window runs from 300 to 1100 ms after the word starts, and the late window runs from 1100 to 1900 ms.
On the bear side, looks to the pear competitor increase steadily as VOT approaches the boundary. This happens in both windows. In the late window, these looks roughly double, from about 0.02 at 0 ms to about 0.04 at 15 ms.
On the pear side, looks to the bear competitor are highest just past the boundary and decrease steadily as VOT moves farther away, toward 40 ms.
Overall, competitor looks are higher in the early window than in the late window. This fits with competition being strongest early on and then resolving as the word is recognized.
So the pattern forms a peak at the category boundary. The closer a sound is to the boundary, the more strongly the other member of the minimal pair is considered.
Section 9: Cascaded Flow from Features to Phonemes

This finding is consistent with the idea that activation flows in a cascaded way from features to phonemes. The model that captures this flow has three layers of processing units.
The feature layer receives a pseudo-spectral input representation. This is a simplified version of the sound’s frequency information. Units in this layer stand for acoustic-phonetic features, such as voicing (VOI) and burst (BUR), along with several other dimensions of the sound.
The phoneme layer contains units for individual phonemes, such as /b/, /r/, /t/, and others.
The word layer contains units for whole words, such as “abrupt.”
Units are copied across many points in time. This lets the model represent sounds and words that start at different moments as the input unfolds.
Within each layer, units that compete with each other inhibit one another, so a strongly active unit pushes down its rivals.
Activity moves upward from features to phonemes and from phonemes to words. It also moves downward from words back to phonemes.
In this model, even stimuli that are identified as /b/ 100% of the time can vary in how strongly and unambiguously the “voiced” feature is activated.
A token with a 0 ms VOT activates “voiced” very strongly.
A token with a 15 ms VOT is still heard as /b/, but it activates “voiced” more weakly. It also gives some activation to “voiceless.”
Consider a stimulus that is obviously +bilabial, meaning it is made with both lips, and obviously +stop, meaning airflow is fully blocked and then released. Both /b/ and /p/ share these two features. They differ only in voicing.
So when the voicing feature is weak or uncertain, the other features fit both phonemes equally well.
This creates some momentary ambiguity between /b/ and /p/. Both phoneme units become partly active for a while.
The partial activation of /p/ passes up to words that begin with /p/, such as “pear.”
That ambiguity is reflected in eye movements while the participant decides where to click. The extra looks to the competitor picture are a visible trace of this brief competition.
Section 10: Partial Activation Can Reach All the Way to the Lexical Level

Partial activation does not stop at the phoneme level. Evidence shows that it can reach all the way to the lexical level, where word meanings are stored.
Andruski et al. (1994) demonstrated that we can even get semantic priming with nonwords.
Semantic priming occurs when hearing one word makes it easier to respond to a related word. For example, hearing “doctor” speeds up a response to “nurse.”
The task was lexical decision. Participants heard a prime and then saw or heard a target. They had to decide as quickly as possible whether the target was a real word. Faster reaction times mean the prime helped.
The primes were words that start with voiceless stops, such as /p/, /t/, or /k/. The researchers shortened the VOT of the first consonant by one third or by two thirds. Shortening VOT pushes the sound toward its voiced partner, such as /b/, /d/, or /g/.
In this way, “peace” moves toward “beace,” and “king” moves toward “ging.” Neither “beace” nor “ging” is a real word.
Even so, “beace” primes “war,” and “ging” primes “queen.” The altered nonword-like token still activates the meaning of the original word.
The study compared two types of items.
In word-to-word items, the voiced counterpart of the prime is also a real word, such as “pet” becoming “bet.” In these items, “bill” is the voiced counterpart of “pill,” and it primes “drug.”
In word-to-nonword items, the voiced counterpart is not a word, such as “king” becoming “ging.”
The reaction time results support this idea.
In every altered condition, responses to related targets were faster than responses after an unrelated prime. Unrelated primes produced the slowest reaction times, close to a full second.
For word-to-nonword items, priming stayed strong even when VOT was cut by two thirds.
For word-to-word items, priming was weaker at the two-thirds level. Here the altered prime sounded more like a different real word, which competed with the intended word.
So lexical representations can be activated even by incomplete phonetic information. A word’s meaning can become active before the system has fully settled on which phoneme it heard.
Section 11: Evidence for Cascaded Processing in Speech Perception

The eye-movement results from McMurray et al. are one piece of evidence for cascaded processing in speech perception.
A thresholded model would have a hard time explaining why the number of looks to the other member of the minimal pair increases gradually as VOT approaches the category boundary.
In a threshold model, the link between the feature level and the phoneme level is blocked until the voicing feature crosses its threshold.
The phoneme level would therefore receive nothing about how close the feature came to the other category. A 15 ms token and a 0 ms token would both simply produce /b/.
If the phoneme level gets no graded information, then the word level cannot get any either. The word “pear” should not become more active for a 15 ms token than for a 0 ms token.
Yet eye movements show exactly that graded difference. Looks to “pear” grow smoothly as VOT nears the boundary.
A cascaded model explains this result naturally. Graded feature activation flows continuously into the phoneme level and then into the word level. The competitor word is partly activated in proportion to how ambiguous the voicing is, and the eyes follow that partial activation.

Section 12: Feedback from the Lexical Layer to the Phoneme Layer

The same model that explains cascaded processing also includes a second property. It allows feedback from the lexical layer, which is the word layer, down to the phoneme layer.
In the model’s structure, arrows run upward from the feature layer to the phoneme layer and from the phoneme layer to the word layer. One arrow also runs downward from the word layer to the phoneme layer.
That downward connection means a word that is becoming active can send activation back to the phonemes it contains. Those phonemes become even more active.
This is the second problem for the simple view from Klatt’s model. In that view, information flows only upward. Word knowledge could never change how a phoneme is perceived.
Feedback is a form of top-down processing. Higher-level knowledge, such as knowing which words exist, influences lower-level processing, such as deciding which sound was heard. Bottom-up processing is the opposite. It is driven purely by the incoming acoustic signal.
Several experiments motivate building this kind of feedback into a model of speech perception.
Section 13: The Ganong Effect: The Basic Design

The Ganong effect is named after Ganong (1980), who studied how phoneme identification is affected by word knowledge. The title “Gisses and kifts for Valentine’s day” is a play on “kisses and gifts” with the first sounds’ voicing swapped. “Kiss” is a word but “giss” is not, while “gift” is a word but “kift” is not.
Ganong created word/nonword pairs. In each pair, the two items differ only in the voicing of the first consonant. One example is “corpse” and “gorpse.” “Corpse” is a real word, but “gorpse” is not.
For each pair, he built a continuum that spanned the relevant phoneme boundary in 5 ms steps of VOT. One end sounded clearly voiced, like /g/, and the other end sounded clearly voiceless, like /k/.
Listeners heard each step and reported whether the first sound was voiced or voiceless.
Two types of continua were compared.
In a word–nonword continuum, the voiced end is a real word, as with “gift” and “kift.”
In a nonword–word continuum, the voiceless end is the real word, as with “giss” and “kiss,” or “gorpse” and “corpse.”
A neutral condition gives a baseline for comparison.
If word knowledge matters, listeners should give more voiced responses when the voiced end is a word. They should give fewer voiced responses when the voiceless end is a word.
The results are measured with two values. The first is the proportion of voiced responses at each step. The second is the lexical effect, which is the difference in voiced responses between the two continuum types at each step. A larger lexical effect means word knowledge pushed responses further.
Section 14: Prediction 1: A Late Correction Process

One possible account is a late “correction” process. In this account, categorical perception works as usual, and phonemes are identified from the sound alone. After that, a separate process adds a bias to report having heard a word.
This account makes a specific prediction about where the lexical effect should appear.
The bias is applied after perception is finished, so it does not depend on how ambiguous the sound was. It acts as a constant push toward the word answer.
So the lexical effect should be the same size at every step of the continuum. In the prediction, it stays flat at about 0.25 across all steps.
The bias would even appear at the clear endpoints. For the word–nonword continuum, listeners would still report a voiced sound about a quarter of the time at the long VOT end, where the sound is clearly voiceless. For the nonword–word continuum, voiced responses would drop to about 0.75 at the short VOT end, where the sound is clearly voiced.
In other words, the word bias would sometimes override even an unambiguous sound.
Section 15: Prediction 2: Word Knowledge Shifts the Category Boundary

A second account says that word knowledge works to shift the categorical boundary itself. In this view, lexical information changes where the dividing line between voiced and voiceless falls.
This account predicts a different pattern.
At the clear endpoints, the sound strongly signals one phoneme. Word knowledge has nothing to add, so the lexical effect should be close to zero.
Near the category boundary, the sound is ambiguous. Here word knowledge can tip the decision, so the lexical effect should be strongest for items closest to the boundary. In the prediction, it peaks at about 0.38 just on either side of the boundary.
The identification curves for the two continuum types would sit on either side of the neutral curve. The word–nonword curve shifts toward more voiced responses, and the nonword–word curve shifts toward fewer. The curves come together again at both ends.
The reasoning is that items closest to the boundary are the ones for which lexical information can help resolve ambiguity about the initial phoneme. When the sound itself is uncertain, the listener’s knowledge of which item is a real word fills in the gap.
Section 16: The Ganong Results

The results are strikingly consistent with the second model, the boundary-shift account.
The lexical effect was small at the endpoints and largest near the category boundary, peaking at about 0.32.
The word–nonword curve gave more voiced responses than the nonword–word curve mainly in the middle of the continuum. The two curves came close together at the short VOT end and the long VOT end.
So listeners did not simply add a fixed word bias to every judgment. Word knowledge mattered most where the sound was most ambiguous.
Ganong raised a number of possibilities for how this could happen. He focused on different kinds of guessing strategies. For example, when a listener is unsure of a sound, they might guess the option that makes a real word.
Later models would emphasize the potential role of feedback in these boundary shifts. In a feedback account, the effect is not a conscious guess. Instead, an active word sends activation down to its phonemes, which changes how the ambiguous sound is perceived.
Section 17: A Simulation of the Ganong Effect in the TRACE Model

The TRACE model is the three-layer model of features, phonemes, and words introduced earlier. It can simulate the Ganong effect. In the simulation, the input begins with a segment that is ambiguous between /b/ and /p/. That segment is followed by /l/, then the vowel in “plug,” then /g/. With /p/, the sequence makes the word “plug.” With /b/, it does not make a word.
The model’s activations can be followed at several points as the input unfolds over time.
Early on, the ambiguous first segment activates /b/ and /p/ equally at the phoneme level. Both are weakly active, and almost no words are active yet.
As /l/ arrives, /b/ and /p/ grow stronger together, along with /l/ and some activation for /r/. At the word level, many words that begin with similar sounds become weakly active at once.
By the third point, “plug” is clearly rising at the word level. However, other words, such as “blood” and “blush,” are also active, since they share the early sounds.
When the final /g/ arrives, activity accumulates strongly for “plug.” The /g/ is highly inconsistent with “blood” and “blush,” so those competitors fall behind. “Plug” becomes the most active word by a wide margin.
At the same time, the phoneme level changes too. /p/ now becomes more active than /b/, even though the acoustic input for that first sound never changed.
This model explains word effects on phoneme category boundaries in terms of top-down processing.
Activity from the lexical level produces feedback to the phoneme level.
That feedback gives greater activation to the phoneme that is consistent with a word. In this case, “plug” sends activation back to /p/, which pulls /p/ ahead of /b/.
So when the input is ambiguous, the word the listener knows helps decide which phoneme they hear. This is the Ganong effect, produced by feedback rather than by a separate guessing strategy.
Section 18: Connection to the Interactive Activation Model

The TRACE model closely resembles an earlier model of reading. TRACE is a version of the Interactive Activation model for written word recognition, developed by McClelland and Rumelhart (1981).
The Interactive Activation model has three levels for reading.
The lowest level detects visual features, such as straight lines and curves at different angles.
The middle level contains letter units, which are activated by matching features.
The top level contains word units, such as WEAR, WORK, WORD, and WEAK.
Activation flows up from features to letters and from letters to words. It also flows down from words back to letters. Units within the same level compete by inhibiting each other.
Partly hidden words show how this works. Suppose someone sees “WOR” followed by a fourth letter that is partly covered. The visible features of that last letter fit both R and K.
The letters W, O, and R strongly activate words such as WORK and WORD.
WORK is a real word that fits the visible features. It sends feedback down to the letter K, which helps K win over R.
So, just as in TRACE, word knowledge resolves ambiguity at a lower level.
Many of the principles are the same in both models. These include graded activation, competition within levels, and feedback from words to smaller units.
However, a key issue in speech perception is the way the stimuli unfold over time. A written word is all on the page at once. A spoken word arrives one sound after another. TRACE handles this by copying its units across many time points, so it can track words that start at different moments as the input comes in.
Section 19: Categorical Perception and the Lexical-Phonetic Interface

The lexical-phonetic interface is the point where sound-level information and word-level information meet. The findings so far change how categorical perception fits into word recognition.
Perception appears to be shaped by category boundaries. Given the same physical difference, it is more difficult to tell apart stimuli from the same category than stimuli from different categories.
Nothing in the findings on cascaded processing or feedback undermines that basic result. Categorical perception is still real.
However, two new conclusions now sit alongside it.
Ambiguity about phoneme identity does have an impact on word recognition. The McMurray eye-tracking study and the Andruski priming study both found that small differences within a category reach the word level. These differences changed looks to competitors and changed priming.
Word-level information seems to have an impact on phoneme categorization. The Ganong effect found that knowing which item is a real word shifts where listeners place the phoneme boundary.
Therefore, it cannot be the case that the word recognition system simply waits for the phoneme recognition system to finish its job, the way a furniture factory waits for lumber from the lumber mill.
Information flows upward continuously, as in cascaded processing. It also flows back down, through feedback.
Spoken word recognition seems to be more interactive than the simple stage-by-stage view.
Section 20: Spoken Word Recognition Is Also Very Fast

Spoken word recognition is also very fast. Allopenna et al. (1994, JML) used a visual world eye-tracking task to measure this speed.
In their task, participants saw a grid with pictures of objects and geometric shapes. They heard instructions to pick up and move one of the objects. The pictures were chosen to compete in specific ways.
The referent was the object actually named, such as a “beaker.”
A cohort competitor shared the beginning of the word, such as “beetle.” Words that start with the same sounds form a cohort, since they are all consistent with the input at first.
A rhyme competitor shared the ending of the word, such as “speaker.”
An unrelated item shared no sounds with the referent, such as “carriage.”
The probability of fixating each item over time after the word began followed a clear pattern.
Looks to the referent began rising at around 200 ms. They climbed steeply and reached nearly 1 by about 800 ms.
Looks to the cohort competitor, “beetle,” rose at the same time as the referent at first. During the early part of the word, “beaker” and “beetle” sound the same. Cohort looks peaked at about 0.2 and then fell once the input ruled “beetle” out.
Looks to the rhyme competitor, “speaker,” rose a little later and stayed smaller, peaking at about 0.1. A rhyme does not match the beginning of the word, but it still overlaps enough to gain some activation.
Looks to the unrelated item stayed near zero the whole time.
These results show the speed of recognition. On average, the spoken word ended at about 375 ms. Yet looks to the referent were already climbing, and the referent was already pulling ahead of the competitors, before the word was finished. Listeners begin narrowing down the word while it is still being spoken.
Section 21: TRACE Predictions Match Eye Movements

The TRACE model’s predicted activations can be compared directly with the eye-movement data from Allopenna et al. Model activation is converted into a predicted fixation probability for each picture.
For the referent, the predicted curve and the actual fixation curve rise together and reach nearly 1 at about the same time.
For the cohort competitor, TRACE predicts an early rise and a peak at about 0.2, followed by a decline. This closely matches the real looks to “beetle.”
For the rhyme competitor, TRACE predicts a smaller and later rise, peaking around 0.1. This also closely matches the real looks to “speaker.”
The simulation has fine granularity. It tracks changes in activation moment by moment, not just the final answer.
Eye movements give us an idea of the changes in “activity” for different competitors over time. The eyes tend to move toward whichever word is currently most active.
So eye-tracking reveals cognitive processing before a final response is issued. It captures the competition among candidate words while it is happening.
A simple choice, such as a mouse click, only reveals the end point.
This point connects back to the graded effects of category goodness in the categorical perception task. In the McMurray study, mouse clicks were categorical, but eye movements revealed that some tokens were better members of their category than others. In the same way, the Allopenna data and TRACE simulations reveal graded, changing activation for competing words over time, which a final response alone would hide.
