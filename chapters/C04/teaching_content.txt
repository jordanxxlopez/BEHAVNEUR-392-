## 1 F B2 Chemicals change behavior by acting on living cells
**Psychopharmacology** studies how drugs change nervous-system activity and behavior. A **drug** is a chemical introduced into the body that changes cellular activity at relatively low amounts. Many drugs act on proteins in the cell membrane. Changing one of these proteins can alter electrical signaling, chemical release, or the cell's response to a neurotransmitter.
A drug's **site of action** is the place where it produces a molecular change. Its **effect** is the resulting physiological or behavioral response. An anesthetic may act on a receptor in the brain while its effect is measured as loss of movement or responsiveness. The receptor and the behavioral response occupy different steps in the mechanism.
General anesthesia depends partly on **microglia**, immune cells that also regulate signaling in brain tissue. In mice, reducing microglial populations delayed the loss of the righting reflex after anesthetic administration. The animals also regained that reflex sooner. The same anesthetic therefore produced a shorter period of behavioral unresponsiveness when this brain-cell population was depleted.
## 2 F B7 A drug and a neurotransmitter can act on the same receptor
A **neurotransmitter** is a chemical released by a neuron that changes another cell's activity. A **receptor** is a protein that binds a chemical signal and changes its own activity in response. Some receptors contain ion channels. Others activate proteins inside the cell, which then regulate enzymes, channels, or release machinery.
**Endogenous** substances are produced within the body; **exogenous** substances enter from outside it. Dopamine released from an axon terminal is endogenous. A drug that activates a dopamine receptor is exogenous. Both can occupy the receptor's binding site, but they may differ in how readily they reach it and how long they remain available.
A drug can also occupy a receptor without activating it. While that drug occupies the binding site, the normal neurotransmitter has fewer available receptors to activate. A receiving neuron can consequently respond less strongly even if the releasing neuron continues to discharge action potentials and deliver the same neurotransmitter into the synaptic gap.
## 3 R PMC10746144 1 Microglial depletion changes the response to anesthesia
Microglia require signaling through **CSF1R**, a receptor that helps these cells survive. He and colleagues fed mice **PLX5622**, a CSF1R inhibitor, to reduce microglial populations. They then administered general anesthetics and timed loss and recovery of the **righting reflex**, the movement that returns an animal from its back to an upright position.
After microglial depletion, mice took longer to lose the righting reflex and recovered it earlier. This pattern occurred with several anesthetics, including pentobarbital, propofol, and ketamine. These compounds do not all act at the same receptor. Their behavioral effects changed after the manipulation of microglia despite their different immediate molecular targets.
The researchers subsequently stopped PLX5622 treatment and allowed microglia to repopulate the brain. Anesthesia induction and emergence returned toward the original responses. Depleting peripheral macrophages with a compound that did not enter the brain failed to reproduce the overall pattern. The altered anesthetic response followed depletion and restoration of brain microglia.
## 4 F B2 Absorption determines how a drug enters circulation
**Pharmacokinetics** describes the movement and removal of a drug through **absorption**, **distribution**, **metabolism**, and **excretion**. Absorption is passage from an administration site into the bloodstream. Before an orally administered drug can affect the brain, it must leave the digestive tract, enter circulation, and survive the chemical processing that occurs along that route.
An **intravenous injection** delivers a drug directly into blood, bypassing absorption across another tissue. Inhaled drugs cross the lungs' thin exchange surfaces. Oral drugs cross the digestive lining, while drugs in a skin patch pass through skin more gradually. These routes differ in the speed at which the drug's blood concentration rises.
Blood from the digestive tract passes through the liver before entering the general circulation. Liver enzymes may chemically modify an absorbed drug during this **first-pass metabolism**. **Bioavailability** is the fraction of the administered substance that reaches systemic circulation. An oral dose can therefore produce less exposure than the same amount injected directly into blood.
## 5 F B3 Distribution determines which tissues a drug reaches
**Distribution** is the movement of a drug from blood into tissues. The bloodstream carries a drug past many organs, but the drug must cross local barriers to reach their cells. Drug molecules bound to blood proteins are temporarily less available to leave circulation. The unbound fraction can move into tissues and approach cellular targets.
The **blood–brain barrier** is formed by specialized brain capillaries that restrict movement from blood into neural tissue. Many lipid-soluble drugs cross cell membranes more readily than strongly charged compounds. Other substances require transport proteins. A drug can be abundant in blood yet enter the brain poorly because it cannot pass these barriers efficiently.
**Metabolism** changes a drug's chemical structure, and **excretion** removes the drug or its products, often through the kidneys. Together they contribute to **clearance**, removal from circulation. A **half-life** describes how long concentration takes to fall by half during a specified elimination phase. Repeated doses can accumulate when administration outpaces removal.
## 6 R PMC9098220 1 Drug access differs between surface and internal receptors
**D1 receptors** are dopamine receptors that activate intracellular signaling through G proteins, proteins that pass signals from receptors to other cellular components. Puri and colleagues examined D1 receptors at the **plasma membrane**, the cell's outer boundary, and at the **Golgi apparatus**, an internal membrane compartment that processes and sorts cellular proteins.

## 7 R PMC9098220 2 A transporter gives dopamine access to an internal target
**Organic cation transporter 2**, or **OCT2**, carries certain positively charged molecules across membranes. Dopamine is one of its substrates. In HeLa cells, a human laboratory cell line, OCT2 occurred at both the plasma membrane and the Golgi. These transport routes allowed externally applied dopamine to reach D1 receptors that were located inside the cell.
The researchers treated cells with **imipramine** to inhibit OCT2-dependent transport. Dopamine then produced much less activation of Golgi D1 receptors. SKF81297 still activated that internal population because it could cross membranes without relying on OCT2. Receptor activation therefore persisted when a membrane-permeant agonist bypassed the inhibited transport step.
OCT2 inhibition changed delivery of the ligand, the chemical that binds the receptor. It did not remove the Golgi receptors or prevent every agonist from activating them. Dopamine needed the transport route to produce the internal response, whereas SKF81297 could reach the same receptor population through its own membrane permeability.
## 8 F B4 Potency and efficacy describe different properties of a drug
A **dose–response curve** relates drug exposure to a measured effect. Within the rising portion, additional drug produces a larger response. The curve eventually levels off when the measured response approaches its maximum. Increasing the dose beyond that point may increase adverse effects even though the desired response becomes little larger.
**Potency** describes the dose needed to produce a specified effect. A more potent drug produces that effect at a lower dose. **Efficacy** describes the maximum effect the drug can produce in the tested system. Two drugs can reach the same maximum while requiring different doses, or differ in both the required dose and maximum response.
**Affinity** is a ligand's tendency to bind a receptor. Binding alone does not determine the final effect: the bound receptor must also activate the relevant cellular processes. A compound may bind strongly but activate a receptor weakly. Its dose–response curve therefore reflects both receptor binding and the response produced after binding occurs.
## 9 R PMC9098220 1 The same receptor class operates in different compartments
Externally applied dopamine activated surface and Golgi D1 receptors in HeLa cells. The membrane-permeant agonist SKF81297 activated Golgi receptors in both cell types. The internal receptor population remained responsive even in cells where dopamine failed to reach it effectively.

Dopamine's chemical structure had not changed. The added transporter supplied a membrane-crossing route that the original cells lacked at a level sufficient to generate the internal response.
Golgi activation also persisted when the researchers blocked **endocytosis**, the process that moves surface membrane and its proteins into the cell. ## 10 F B5 Desired and adverse effects can rise at different doses
A **therapeutic effect** is a desired treatment response; an **adverse effect** is an unwanted response. Morphine can reduce pain while also depressing respiration. These effects involve opioid-sensitive neural pathways that perform different functions. The dose that produces useful pain relief and the dose that dangerously suppresses breathing need not be identical.
A **margin of safety** describes the separation between exposures producing the desired and dangerous effects. A wider separation allows more variation in exposure before severe toxicity develops. When the curves lie close together, a relatively small increase can move a patient from a therapeutic response toward serious respiratory or other physiological impairment.
The same administered amount can produce different exposure in different people. Slower elimination leaves more active drug available, while another sedating substance may add to respiratory depression. With repeated doses, residual drug can remain when the next dose arrives. The concentration can rise across doses even when the amount in each individual dose stays constant.
## 11 R PMC8208814 1 An opioid receptor's location changes its active state

In cultured PC12 cells, some newly synthesized DOR remained in an intracellular compartment. The membrane-permeant agonist **SNC80** could reach both the surface population and this internal receptor population.

## 12 F B6 Repeated exposure can decrease or increase a response
**Tolerance** is a reduced response after repeated drug exposure. The same dose now produces less effect than it did initially. One cause is faster drug metabolism, which lowers exposure. Another is reduced cellular responsiveness, such as fewer receptors at the surface or weaker signaling after those receptors are activated by the drug.
**Sensitization** is an increased response after repeated exposure. Particular stimulant effects can become larger across treatments even while other effects develop tolerance. These terms describe changes in specified responses, not a single condition affecting every drug action equally. Movement, reward-related behavior, sedation, and autonomic responses can adapt at different rates within the same animal.
Repeated exposure can also produce **physical dependence**, in which cellular adjustments compensate for the drug's continuing action. Removing the drug leaves those adjustments temporarily unopposed. The resulting **withdrawal symptoms** may oppose the original drug effects. A nervous system adapted to persistent suppression can become unusually excitable when the suppressing substance is suddenly withdrawn.
## 13 R PMC9708073 2 Opioid exposure removes receptors from an axon's surface
Jullié and colleagues studied **mu opioid receptors**, or **MORs**, in cultured striatal neurons. Activating these presynaptic receptors inhibited synaptic vesicle release. After prolonged exposure to the MOR agonist **DAMGO**, a fresh agonist challenge inhibited release less effectively. The neurons had developed tolerance to this particular presynaptic action of the opioid.
DAMGO also moved MORs from the axon surface into **endosomes**, membrane-bound compartments inside the cell. Surface-labeled receptors accumulated beside an endosomal marker. This movement reduced the receptor population available to extracellular agonist. A terminal with fewer accessible MORs could maintain less opioid-mediated inhibition when electrically stimulated to release transmitter.
MOR uptake required **phosphorylation**, the addition of phosphate groups to the receptor's intracellular tail. Removing selected phosphorylation sites strongly reduced uptake and prevented the usual development of tolerance. Inhibiting the enzymes GRK2 and GRK3 also reduced both processes. These enzymes normally add phosphate groups that promote removal of the receptor from the axon surface.
## 14 R PMC9708073 4 Opioid receptor subtypes differ in their recycling
After entering an endosome, a receptor can return to the plasma membrane through **recycling**. Receptor-containing vesicles fuse with the membrane and restore receptors to the surface. This return competes with continuing uptake. Surface availability therefore depends on how quickly receptors leave the membrane and how quickly internal receptors are delivered back.
Both mu and delta opioid receptors underwent agonist-dependent uptake in cultured axons. Delta receptors returned to the surface less efficiently than mu receptors. During prolonged treatment, the delta receptor population consequently became more depleted at the axon surface. Delta-mediated inhibition of vesicle release also developed a larger loss of responsiveness in this preparation.
Tolerance remained selective for the activated receptor subtype. In neurons expressing both receptors, prolonged exposure to a mu-selective agonist reduced the mu response while preserving the delta response. A delta-selective agonist produced the converse pattern. The terminal retained its capacity for opioid inhibition through the receptor population that had not undergone the same prolonged activation.
## 15 F B5 Expectations and conditioning change measured responses
A **placebo** resembles a treatment but lacks the active component being evaluated. A **placebo response** is a change that occurs in that treatment setting. Expectations and learned associations can change pain processing and reported symptoms. Such effects involve nervous-system activity even when the administered substance does not contain the tested analgesic drug.
Pain-relieving expectations can activate endogenous pain-control systems, including pathways that use opioid messengers. A previously effective treatment can also make its associated setting or cue acquire an effect. After that learning, the cue can influence later responses. The chemical action of a drug and the nervous system's response to treatment cues can occur together.
In a **blinded comparison**, participants or evaluators do not know which treatment was given. A matched placebo preserves features such as pill appearance and administration routine. Comparing the active drug with that condition reduces differences caused by treatment expectations. The remaining difference estimates the added response produced when the active chemical is present.

## 18 R PMC9098220 4 Internal D1 receptors contribute to a second messenger
Activated D1 receptors stimulate **Gs**, a G protein that increases activity of the enzyme **adenylyl cyclase**. ## 19 F B7 Drugs can alter several stages of synaptic communication
At a chemical synapse, the presynaptic neuron synthesizes transmitter and loads it into vesicles. An action potential opens calcium channels in the terminal. Calcium entry triggers vesicle fusion, releasing transmitter into the synaptic gap. The transmitter then binds receptors on the receiving cell or on the releasing terminal itself.
Drugs can intervene before transmitter reaches a receptor. Blocking a synthetic enzyme reduces production of new transmitter. Blocking vesicle loading leaves less transmitter available for release. Preventing vesicle fusion reduces the amount entering the gap after an action potential. Each intervention decreases chemical delivery through a different molecular step in the releasing neuron.
After release, transporters and enzymes help terminate the signal. Blocking reuptake leaves transmitter outside cells for longer, while blocking its breakdown preserves molecules that would otherwise be destroyed. A receptor agonist instead activates the receiving protein directly. A receptor antagonist blocks activation even when the normal transmitter has been released into the gap.
## 20 F B12 Proteins and precursors supply chemical signaling
Neurons need raw materials to make chemical signals. A **precursor** is a starting substance that an enzyme changes into another molecule. Acetylcholine, for example, is made from choline and an acetyl group supplied by acetyl coenzyme A. The enzyme **choline acetyltransferase** joins these components, producing transmitter that can then be loaded into vesicles.
Reducing the supply of a precursor can reduce transmitter production. Blocking the enzyme that performs the chemical reaction can have a similar effect, even when the raw materials remain available. Drugs acting at these steps change how much transmitter the neuron can make, rather than directly activating or blocking receptors on the receiving cell.
Receptors and enzymes are themselves proteins that neurons must produce. After a new receptor is made, it must travel to the membrane where it will function. In cultured neurons, fluorescent labeling allows newly produced glutamate-receptor cargo to be followed as it moves through dendrites. Chemical communication therefore requires both transmitter production and delivery of the proteins that detect it.
## 21 F B7 Vesicle storage and release offer presynaptic targets
Making a transmitter does not immediately make it available for release. Many transmitters must first enter **synaptic vesicles**, small membrane-bound sacs inside an axon terminal. Transport proteins move transmitter into these sacs. A drug that blocks this loading step can leave a terminal with transmitter in its cytoplasm but little packaged for release.
An arriving action potential opens voltage-sensitive calcium channels in the terminal membrane. Calcium enters and activates proteins that help a vesicle fuse with the membrane. Fusion opens a route from the vesicle interior to the synaptic gap. The released transmitter can then reach receptors on another neuron, a muscle cell, or a gland.
Botulinum toxin interferes with proteins required for vesicle fusion. At a motor nerve terminal, this prevents acetylcholine release, so the muscle receives less chemical stimulation and contracts less. Black widow spider venom instead triggers extensive transmitter release. These substances change communication before the transmitter binds a receptor on the receiving cell.

Receptors must reach the cell surface before they can respond to transmitter outside the neuron. An **AMPA receptor** is a glutamate-sensitive ion channel that contributes to excitatory signaling. ## 24 F B8 Agonists and antagonists alter receptor activation
An agonist binds a receptor and promotes the change that activates it. An **antagonist** binds without producing that activation and reduces the response to an agonist. At a transmitter's binding site, these molecules compete for access. When more receptors are occupied by antagonist, fewer are available for the transmitter to activate.
An **allosteric site** is a different binding location on the same receptor. A molecule acting there can change how readily the receptor responds without occupying the transmitter's own binding site. Its effect may increase or decrease receptor activity. The receptor's response therefore depends on both the transmitter and substances acting at these additional sites.
Receptor activation and receptor number are also separate sources of change. Blocking a receptor reduces signaling while the receptor remains present. Removing receptors from the surface reduces the number available to respond. Inserting additional receptors can increase the receiving cell's sensitivity even when the amount of transmitter released into the synaptic gap stays unchanged.
## 25 R PMC11326776 2 Synaptic stimulation changes local receptor transport
A **dendritic spine** is a small projection that receives an excitatory synaptic contact. Its receptors help determine how strongly it responds to released glutamate. The receptor-transport study stimulated a selected spine by using light to release glutamate from an inactive chemical form. This method activated a small region rather than stimulating the entire neuron equally.
After stimulation, the targeted spine enlarged and contained more labeled GluA1. Nearby receptor-containing vesicles also changed their movement within the dendrite. They made fewer long directed runs and moved less freely through the surrounding region. Receptor cargo that had previously traveled along the branch became more restricted near the stimulated synapse.
This restriction occurred mainly near the stimulated spine, while more distant dendritic regions retained their earlier movement patterns. Local activity can therefore change receptor delivery within one part of a neuron. The cell does not have to alter every synapse in the same way when a particular input undergoes a sustained increase in strength.
## 26 F B7 Receptor delivery changes a cell's responsiveness
A receptor inside a transport vesicle cannot yet detect transmitter in the synaptic gap. For that receptor to become available, the vesicle must reach the surface and fuse with the cell membrane. The receptor is then part of the membrane, with its transmitter-binding region exposed outside the cell and its signaling regions facing the appropriate cellular compartments.
The number of available receptors can change as insertion and removal continue. More insertion can increase the surface population; more removal can reduce it. These changes alter how much response a given amount of transmitter produces. A receiving neuron can therefore become more or less sensitive without requiring a matching change in transmitter release.
Cargo also moves through the cell's crowded interior, where protein structures can restrict its path. Recordings of small particles inside dendrites make this movement visible. Some particles move over longer distances, while others remain within a smaller area. Such physical restrictions help explain why delivery depends on the organization of the cell interior as well as receptor production.

## 28 R PMC11326776 3 Actin and myosin help retain and deliver receptor cargo
**Actin** is a protein that assembles into filaments, forming part of the **cytoskeleton**, the cell's internal framework. In the receptor-transport study, synaptic stimulation increased actin filaments in the nearby dendrite. ## 29 F B9 Glutamate activates several receptor classes
**Glutamate** is the main excitatory neurotransmitter in the mammalian brain. At many synapses, it opens **AMPA receptors**, allowing positive ions to enter the receiving neuron. This makes the membrane less negative and increases the chance that other excitatory inputs will help trigger an action potential. Changing the number of AMPA receptors changes the strength of this response.
**NMDA receptors** also respond to glutamate, but their channels are blocked by magnesium when the membrane is sufficiently negative. Depolarization helps relieve that block. Glutamate and the appropriate electrical condition must therefore occur together for substantial current to pass. The open channel admits calcium as well as other ions, allowing electrical activity to initiate changes inside the cell.
Calcium can activate enzymes that alter synaptic strength and receptor delivery. Glutamate also acts on **metabotropic receptors**, which use G proteins rather than containing the ion channel themselves. Drugs can act differently at these receptor families. Ketamine, for example, blocks NMDA receptor channels, changing one part of glutamate signaling rather than eliminating every response to the transmitter.
## 30 R PMC11326776 5 Receptor vesicles fuse near a stimulated synapse
Receptor delivery is completed when a cargo vesicle fuses with the neuronal membrane, a process called **exocytosis**. The AMPA-receptor researchers used a fluorescent tag that was dim inside an acidic vesicle and bright after exposure to the fluid outside the cell. Each brief brightening identified a receptor-insertion event at the surface.
Synaptic stimulation increased these insertion events, especially near the stimulated spine. Nearby vesicles had already become more restricted in their movement through the dendrite. The neuron could therefore draw on a local supply of receptor cargo while adding receptor proteins to its surface. Delivery changed most strongly in the region where the synapse had been activated.
Disrupting actin assembly or inhibiting myosin reduced the increase in receptor insertion. Actin helped retain cargo near the active region, while myosin contributed to the later delivery process. A stronger synaptic response can consequently involve movement and membrane insertion of receptor proteins, in addition to changes in how receptors already at the synapse function.
## 31 F B10 GABA controls excitability through channels and G proteins
**GABA** is a major inhibitory neurotransmitter in the mature brain. It is produced from glutamate by an enzyme inside GABA-releasing neurons. After release, GABA can activate **GABA-A receptors**, which contain channels permeable mainly to chloride. Opening these channels changes the membrane's electrical behavior and usually reduces the ability of excitatory inputs to trigger firing.
The effect depends on the chloride gradient, the difference in chloride concentration across the membrane. Opening the channel moves voltage toward the value favored by that gradient. In mature neurons, the resulting current commonly opposes excitation or makes it harder for other inputs to change voltage enough to generate an action potential.
**GABA-B receptors** work differently because they activate G proteins instead of directly containing the chloride channel. Their signaling can open potassium channels or reduce calcium entry. At a releasing terminal, less calcium entry can reduce transmitter release. GABA can therefore suppress communication through both an immediate channel response and slower changes produced through intracellular signaling.

## 33 F B10 Allosteric drugs modify how a receptor responds
An allosteric drug changes a receptor's response by binding outside the transmitter's own binding site. The GABA-A receptor has several such sites. **Benzodiazepines** enhance the receptor's response to GABA, increasing the inhibitory influence of GABAergic signaling. They do not simply replace GABA at its usual binding location on the receptor.
**Barbiturates** act at a different site and can also increase GABA-A channel activity. Because the receptor contains several interacting binding regions, different drugs can modify the same channel through different molecular contacts. The resulting inhibition depends on which substance is present, its concentration, and how it changes the receptor's opening behavior.
The receptor's chloride channel can also be blocked. **Picrotoxin** interferes with channel function, reducing the inhibitory current even when GABA is available. Enhancing a receptor's response and blocking its channel therefore push signaling in opposite directions. These actions change communication by modifying a protein already present in the receiving membrane, rather than changing GABA synthesis or release.

## 36 F B11 Acetylcholine acts through two major receptor families
**Acetylcholine**, or **ACh**, is released onto skeletal muscles and within several brain pathways. Drugs that inhibit this enzyme prolong acetylcholine's availability. They change the duration of signaling without having to activate either receptor family directly or increase the neuron's initial transmitter release.

## 39 F B15 Catecholamine synthesis creates a sequence of drug targets
**Catecholamines** are a group of related chemical messengers that includes dopamine, norepinephrine, and epinephrine. Neurons build them through a sequence of enzyme-controlled reactions. The amino acid **tyrosine** is first changed into **L-DOPA** by tyrosine hydroxylase. Another enzyme then converts L-DOPA into dopamine, which can be packaged into vesicles and released.
In norepinephrine-producing cells, an additional enzyme converts dopamine into norepinephrine. The final transmitter therefore depends on which enzymes the cell makes. Blocking an early reaction can reduce the supply of several later products, whereas blocking a later reaction affects a narrower part of the sequence. A synthetic pathway consequently contains several distinct places where a drug can act.
L-DOPA is used to increase dopamine production in Parkinson's disease because it can enter the brain more readily than dopamine itself. Once inside, it can be converted into dopamine by the remaining enzymatic step. Giving a precursor increases the material available for transmitter synthesis, rather than activating dopamine receptors directly in the way a receptor agonist does.

## 45 F B17 Reuptake and enzymes terminate monoamine signals
After a monoamine transmitter is released, **reuptake transporters** move much of it back into cells. ## 56 F B7 Peptide transmitters require different packaging

The response still depends on the receiving cell: a peptide changes the activity of a particular pathway through the receptors expressed there.

They also measured responses to a uniform background before presenting visible targets. These conditions established how often a rapid movement occurred without a detectable dot, providing a comparison for the later avoidance behavior under different visual conditions.
## 69 R PMC4987138 8 CB1 activation improves vision under dim illumination
Under **mesopic** conditions, an intermediate range of dim illumination, untreated tadpoles often failed to avoid the moving dots reliably. Tadpoles treated with WIN responded more successfully under the same low-light conditions. The drug therefore changed performance when the visual target was difficult to detect, rather than requiring a different kind of stimulus or movement response.
The researchers also recorded stronger light-evoked firing in retinal ganglion cells after cannabinoid receptor activation. These neurons supply visual information to the brain, so changing their response can alter the sensory signal available before an avoidance movement is selected. The behavioral improvement occurred alongside a measured change in the output neurons of the visual system.
Ordinary swimming speed between dot encounters was similar in control and treated animals. Treated tadpoles, however, produced faster movements during successful escapes. Increasing endogenous cannabinoid availability also improved dim-light avoidance, and blocking CB1 prevented that benefit. Both receptor activation and preservation of the body's own cannabinoid messenger could therefore change performance in this particular visual task.
## 70 R PMC4987138 6 Cannabinoids change chloride-dependent excitability
The retinal study identified a change in chloride handling inside ganglion cells. CB1 activation reduced intracellular chloride through a pathway involving **NKCC1**, a transporter that normally brings chloride into the cell. Less chloride accumulated inside, changing the electrical effect of chloride-permeable channels. Fluorescent chloride measurements allowed this internal ion change to be followed during drug exposure.
The shift made the resting membrane potential more negative through chloride currents controlled by **glycine**, another neurotransmitter. This hyperpolarization increased the availability of sodium channels for later activation. When the cell subsequently received a depolarizing stimulus, it could produce more action potentials. A more negative resting voltage therefore occurred together with a stronger response to a later input in these retinal neurons.
The researchers observed hyperpolarization in isolated ganglion cells as well as altered responses in the retina. Blocking CB1 prevented the voltage change, and blocking glycine receptors prevented the usual increase in visual responsiveness. The chemical effect involved the receiving cell's ion transport and channels, which changed how visual input was converted into firing before the signal traveled onward to the brain.
## 71 R PMC4179426 6 Receptor activation changes the mechanical state of a cell
Cannabinoid signaling can also change the shape of the cell body. Roland and colleagues examined **Neuro2A cells**, a cultured neural cell line that grows identifiable processes. After CB1 activation, these processes retracted and the cell body became rounder. Actin accumulated beneath the membrane and near the ends of the retracting processes as the cell changed shape.
The researchers measured **stiffness**, the cell's resistance to being deformed. A small probe pressed against the cell surface, allowing that resistance to be compared before and after treatment. CB1 activation produced a temporary increase in stiffness alongside the structural changes. The response therefore included a measurable change in the cell's physical properties, not only a change in its outline.
Inhibiting myosin with blebbistatin prevented the usual cell-rounding response. Myosin normally generates force against the actin network beneath the membrane, so reducing its activity weakens the contraction. The agonist could still reach the receptor, but the physical response was interrupted downstream. Receptor signaling had changed cell shape by engaging an internal network capable of producing mechanical force.
## 72 R PMC9708073 3 Agonists differ in receptor uptake and cellular tolerance
Agonists that activate the same opioid receptor can differ in how strongly they promote its removal from the surface. DAMGO produced substantial mu-receptor uptake in cultured axons. Morphine produced little detectable rapid uptake, yet prolonged morphine exposure still reduced receptor availability and weakened presynaptic inhibition. Its slower effects differed from those measured immediately after the drug was applied.
Two other agonists, **PZM21** and **TRV130**, produced little detectable receptor uptake and did not produce the same tolerance after the tested prolonged treatment. These compounds favored some receptor-signaling responses more than others. An agonist's ability to activate a receptor initially therefore need not match its ability to promote the cellular processes that later reduce responsiveness.
Blocking GRK2 and GRK3 also prevented the tolerance produced by prolonged morphine treatment in this preparation. These enzymes add phosphate groups that regulate the receptor's movement and availability. The later loss of opioid responsiveness depended partly on that regulatory process, even though morphine had produced little measurable rapid uptake during the shorter initial observation period.
## 73 R PMC4179426 7 Cannabinoids reshape several neuronal compartments
Cannabinoids can change neuronal shape by activating **CB1 receptors**. Researchers examined cultured hippocampal neurons and followed changes in the cell body and **dendrites**, the branches that receive signals from other neurons. These structures form the **somatodendritic region**. After receptor activation, outer dendritic branches retracted while portions nearer the cell body broadened, changing the shape of this region within minutes.
These changes involve **actin**, a protein that forms part of the **cytoskeleton**, the internal framework that supports the cell and helps it change shape. CB1 activation reorganized actin into straighter bundles as nearby microtubules bent. Following the same neuron over several minutes revealed how this internal reorganization accompanied physical changes in its dendrites and the region around its cell body.
Brief activation changed shape within minutes, while overnight treatment reduced the number of developing dendrites. Researchers examined these outcomes separately because immediate contraction and longer-term growth are different processes. Blocking ROCK or myosin prevented the overnight reduction. The chemical changed neuronal structure through a pathway that regulated the cell's internal framework and the development of its branches over different timescales.
