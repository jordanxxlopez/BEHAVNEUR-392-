## 1 F B2 Chemicals change behavior by acting on living cells
**Psychopharmacology** examines how drugs change nervous-system function and behavior. A **drug** is a chemical introduced from outside the body that alters cellular activity at relatively low amounts. Its behavioral effect depends on the cells it reaches, the molecules it acts on, and the signals those cells send to other targets.
A drug's **site of action** is the molecular or cellular location where it produces a change. Its **effect** is the resulting alteration in physiology or behavior. A sleeping animal, a slower heart, and a reduced pain response are observable outcomes; the proteins that produced those outcomes occupy particular places inside the body.
The same chemical can produce different responses in different preparations. General anesthetics provide a concrete example: their effects depend on neural circuits and supporting cells as well as the administered compound. In mice, changing microglial populations alters the transition into and out of anesthesia, placing a drug response within its living tissue environment.
## 2 F B7 A drug and a neurotransmitter can act on the same receptor
A **neurotransmitter** is a chemical released by a neuron that changes the activity of a receiving cell. A **receptor** is a protein that recognizes a chemical signal and initiates a response. A drug can activate or block a receptor normally used by a messenger produced within the nervous system.
**Endogenous** substances originate within the body, whereas **exogenous** substances enter from outside it. Dopamine released from a nerve terminal is endogenous. A compound administered to activate dopamine receptors is exogenous. Their origin differs even when both affect the same receptor population and influence some of the same cellular processes.
A receptor does not recognize a chemical according to its purpose as medicine or recreational drug. Binding depends on molecular properties, and the response depends on the receptor and receiving cell. Explaining a drug effect therefore requires identifying the target protein and the cellular changes that follow its activation or inhibition.

## 4 F B2 Absorption determines how a drug enters circulation
**Pharmacokinetics** describes how a drug moves through the body and is removed from it. Its major processes are **absorption**, **distribution**, **metabolism**, and **excretion**. Absorption carries a substance from its administration site into the bloodstream, beginning the route by which it can reach tissues distant from where it entered.
The **route of administration** changes the tissues a drug encounters before reaching the blood. An oral drug must be released and absorbed from the digestive tract, while an intravenous injection enters circulation directly. Inhaled substances cross lung surfaces, and some compounds pass gradually through skin from a controlled delivery system.
After oral absorption, blood from the digestive tract passes through the liver before reaching much of the body. **Metabolism** chemically modifies a drug, sometimes producing an active metabolite. This **first-pass metabolism** changes **bioavailability**, the fraction of the administered substance reaching systemic circulation, so swallowed and injected amounts need not produce the same exposure.
## 5 F B3 Distribution determines which tissues a drug reaches
**Distribution** is the movement of a drug from circulation into tissues. Blood delivers a substance around the body, but entry into a tissue depends on molecular properties, transport proteins, and the barriers at that location. A high concentration in plasma does not guarantee an equally high concentration beside a receptor in the brain.
The **blood–brain barrier** restricts passage from brain capillaries into neural tissue. Many lipid-soluble molecules enter more readily than strongly charged molecules, while selected transporters carry particular compounds across membranes. Binding to blood proteins also changes the fraction available to leave circulation and reach other tissues during a given interval.
**Excretion** removes a drug or its metabolites from the body, often through the kidneys. Metabolism and excretion together influence **clearance**, the removal of active substance from circulation. A **half-life** is the time required for concentration to fall by half during a specified elimination phase, helping describe how exposure changes after administration stops.

## 8 F B4 Potency and efficacy describe different properties of a drug
A **dose–response curve** relates an administered amount or concentration to a measured effect. The rising portion indicates that increasing exposure produces a larger response within that range. The curve can level off when the measured system approaches its maximum response, even though adding more drug may still affect other targets.
**Potency** describes how much drug is required to produce a specified effect. A more potent compound reaches that effect at a lower dose under the tested conditions. **Efficacy** describes the maximum effect a compound can produce in that system, so greater potency does not necessarily mean a larger maximum response.
**Affinity** describes a ligand's tendency to bind a receptor, while potency also depends on exposure and how receptor activation produces the measured effect. Two compounds can differ in potency for movement, pain, or cellular signaling. Comparing dose–response curves therefore requires keeping the preparation, measurement, and exposure conditions explicit rather than calling one compound stronger in every respect.

## 10 F B5 Desired and adverse effects can rise at different doses
A **therapeutic effect** is a desired change produced by treatment, while an **adverse effect** is an unwanted change. The same drug can produce both because its targets occur in several tissues or because one target regulates several functions. Separate dose–response curves describe how each measured outcome changes with exposure.
A **therapeutic window** is the exposure range in which useful effects can occur without unacceptable adverse effects. The separation depends on the drug, the person or preparation, and the outcomes being compared. A compound can strongly activate its target while offering little separation between its desired and unwanted physiological consequences.
Receptor location helps explain this problem. Opioid-sensitive pathways influence pain processing, breathing, and other functions, so activating the broader system produces more than one outcome. Comparing responses across doses and tissues is necessary to understand the available separation between effects, rather than assuming that selective receptor binding guarantees selective behavior.

## 12 F B6 Repeated exposure can decrease or increase a response
**Tolerance** develops when repeated exposure produces a smaller response, so a previously effective dose has less effect. Cells can change receptor availability, signaling proteins, or other processes during continued treatment. Metabolism can also change, altering the exposure produced by the same administered amount before it reaches its targets.
**Sensitization** is an increase in a response after repeated exposure. A drug can produce tolerance in one measured effect and sensitization in another because the underlying circuits and adaptations differ. Describing repeated administration therefore requires naming the particular outcome rather than assigning one universal direction of change to the whole drug.
**Withdrawal** occurs when stopping or reducing a repeatedly used drug exposes adaptations that developed during its presence. Compensatory processes can temporarily push function away from its usual range when the compound is removed. The resulting symptoms depend on the affected systems and the history of exposure, rather than being identical for every psychoactive substance.

## 15 F B5 Expectations and conditioning change measured responses
A **placebo** resembles a treatment but lacks the active component whose effect is being tested. A **placebo response** is a change occurring in that treatment setting, which may include expectation, learned associations, and other processes. Such changes can involve measurable physiology rather than requiring a person to invent an experience.
An expected reduction in pain can recruit endogenous systems that regulate pain processing. Prior experience with treatment can also make a contextual cue influence later responses. The setting therefore contributes to the measured outcome alongside any direct molecular action of the administered compound, especially when the outcome includes perception or reported symptoms.
A treatment comparison must account for these influences to identify what the active compound contributes. A blinded procedure limits differences in expectations between conditions, while a matched placebo preserves much of the treatment context. The resulting contrast estimates the drug's added effect within that setting rather than treating every observed improvement as its direct chemical action.

## 19 F B7 Drugs can alter several stages of synaptic communication
At a chemical synapse, a neuron synthesizes a transmitter, stores it, and releases it toward a receiving cell. Receptors detect the released chemical, and transporters or enzymes help terminate its action. Each stage contains proteins that an exogenous compound can alter, creating several routes to changing the strength or duration of communication.
A drug can increase signaling without directly activating the receiving receptor. It might supply a synthetic precursor, increase release, block a presynaptic inhibitory receptor, or slow removal of the transmitter. Conversely, reducing synthesis or release can weaken transmission before the chemical ever reaches its usual postsynaptic binding site.
The resulting effect depends on the direction of the altered step. Blocking a transporter that clears a transmitter differs from blocking a receptor that detects it. Naming only the transmitter system therefore leaves the mechanism incomplete; the specific target and its position in the communication sequence determine how the compound changes signaling.
## 20 F B12 Proteins and precursors supply chemical signaling
Cells manufacture the proteins needed for chemical signaling, including receptors and enzymes that synthesize transmitters. An **enzyme** accelerates a chemical reaction, while a **precursor** supplies material for that reaction. Changing enzyme activity or precursor availability can alter the transmitter available for later storage and release without directly activating the postsynaptic receptor.
**Acetylcholine** is synthesized from choline and acetyl coenzyme A by **choline acetyltransferase**. Other systems use different precursors and enzymes, so one synthesis inhibitor does not automatically reduce every transmitter. For example, catecholamine production begins with tyrosine, while **gamma-aminobutyric acid**, or **GABA**, is produced from glutamate by a different enzyme.
Receptor proteins also require synthesis and delivery to useful cellular locations. Newly made AMPA-receptor components travel within dendrites before insertion into the surface. The unstimulated transport preparation makes this ongoing supply visible, placing receptor availability beside transmitter production as another cellular process that determines how strongly a neuron can communicate through a chemical signal.
## 21 F B7 Vesicle storage and release offer presynaptic targets
**Synaptic vesicles** store many classical neurotransmitters near release sites in axon terminals. Transport proteins load the vesicles using energy-dependent gradients. A drug that prevents loading can reduce the amount available for release, even when the terminal still synthesizes the transmitter and can generate its usual electrical activity.
An arriving action potential opens voltage-dependent calcium channels. Calcium then activates proteins that make selected vesicles fuse with the terminal membrane and release their contents. Blocking calcium entry or disrupting the fusion machinery can reduce transmitter output without requiring the receiving cell's receptors to be blocked directly by the drug.
**Botulinum toxin** interferes with proteins needed for acetylcholine release at affected terminals. Its mechanism differs from a receptor antagonist acting on the receiving muscle, although both can weaken muscle activation. Identifying whether a compound acts before or after release explains how superficially similar outcomes can arise through different cellular targets.

## 24 F B8 Agonists and antagonists alter receptor activation

The agonist, antagonist, receptor state, and receiving cell together determine the physiological result.

## 31 F B10 GABA controls excitability through channels and G proteins
**GABA** is a major inhibitory neurotransmitter in the mature mammalian brain. **GABA-A receptors** are ligand-gated channels that increase membrane conductance to chloride and related anions. Their effect depends on the ions' electrochemical gradients, so the channel's influence is determined by the receiving cell's conditions as well as by transmitter binding.
Increasing inhibitory conductance can pull voltage toward the anion equilibrium potential or reduce the effect of concurrent excitatory current. This reduction of voltage change is often called **shunting inhibition**. **GABA-B receptors** use G proteins and act through slower signaling processes, including changes in potassium and calcium channels. Presynaptic GABA-B activity can reduce transmitter release, while postsynaptic activity can reduce excitability. Receptor subtype identifies the mechanism, preventing the mistaken assumption that every GABA effect is produced by one identical chloride channel.

## 73 R PMC4179426 7 Cannabinoids reshape several neuronal compartments
The cannabinoid-growth study also examined the **somatodendritic region**, which includes the cell body and dendrites, in cultured hippocampal neurons. Acute receptor activation changed dendritic shape and actin organization. The response extended the structural mechanism beyond the axonal growth cone, placing the same signaling system within several recognizable compartments of a developing neuron.
The investigators distinguished rapid remodeling from effects measured after longer exposure or interference with contractile machinery. The timescale of treatment changed which structural outcome was being examined. A receptor-driven process can therefore contribute to an immediate mechanical response and to later neuronal organization through related downstream components operating over different periods.
The study brings drug action back to a complete causal sequence: a compound reaches a receptor, the receptor recruits intracellular signaling, effectors alter cellular function, and the changed cell contributes to a larger neural system. Identifying each step explains why access, target location, exposure history, and receiving-cell machinery all matter when describing how chemicals change behavior.
