## I01|F|book|6.6|A blind spot interrupts the retinal image
The **optic disc** is the location where ganglion-cell axons leave the retina to form the optic nerve. It contains no rods or cones. Light falling there cannot activate photoreceptors, producing a small blind region within each eye's visual field even when the surrounding retina is functioning normally.
In the original demonstration, keeping one eye closed and fixating the cross can make the separate colored circle disappear at a particular viewing distance. Changing distance moves the circle's retinal image onto or away from the optic disc. The disappearance occurs while the cross remains visible because the two images occupy different retinal locations.
The missing region usually escapes awareness during ordinary viewing. The other eye can supply overlapping information, and the surrounding visual pattern influences what is perceived at the blind location. The continuous appearance of a scene therefore does not mean that every point in it was sampled by a photoreceptor in each eye.

## I02|F|book|6.15|Surrounding light changes apparent brightness
Two patches reflecting the same amount of light can look different when their surroundings differ. A patch surrounded by darkness often appears brighter than the same patch surrounded by a bright region. **Brightness contrast** depends on comparisons between neighboring image regions rather than only the amount of light reaching one small retinal location.
Center-surround receptive fields provide one source of such comparisons. A bright surround can oppose the activity driven by an ON center, while a darker surround produces less opposition. Different surrounding stimulation can therefore change a cell's output even when the physical light falling in its center remains constant.
Perceived brightness also depends on larger-scale cortical interpretations of surfaces and illumination. A visible boundary may represent a change in paint, a shadow, or a change in surface orientation. Local retinal comparisons provide contrast information, while additional processing helps determine how that contrast belongs to the objects and lighting of the scene.

## I03|R|PMC8580503|1|A static starburst can appear to emit bright rays
The **Scintillating Starburst** is an original visual stimulus built from overlapping concentric star polygons. Many observers perceive fleeting bright rays extending outward from its center. Those extended rays are not drawn as continuous bright lines in the physical image; their appearance changes as the observer inspects the pattern.
Karlovich and Wallisch tested how the arrangement of the polygons affected the reported rays. Contrast, the number of repeated rings, and the pattern's intersections changed the perceptual effect. The experiment therefore related a specific visual experience to controlled changes in an existing image rather than merely presenting an unusual decorative pattern.
The authors proposed an interaction between central and peripheral visual processing. Intersections provide local signals that can be interpreted differently when sampled away from fixation, contributing to apparent extended brightness. This explanation concerns how the pattern may be processed; the directly measured result was the observers' reported strength of the illusory rays.

## I04|R|PMC8580503|3|Grid patterns expose interactions across visual space
In grid illusions, brightness can appear at locations where no corresponding spot or line was printed. The original starburst study compared its stimulus with several grid patterns, including the **pincushion grid**. Their arrangements differ, but each places repeated intersections and neighboring contours across central and peripheral visual space.
Moving fixation changes which intersections fall on central retina and which fall on more broadly pooled peripheral regions. Apparent features can strengthen, weaken, or disappear during inspection. The physical pattern remains the same while the retinal sampling and the balance of surrounding signals change with each new gaze position.
The authors compared the starburst and grid patterns to distinguish their properties rather than treating all of them as one illusion. Similar-looking percepts need not have identical causes. Testing how contrast and geometry change each effect provides more specific evidence than assuming that any apparent brightness at an intersection must follow one simple retinal explanation.

## I05|R|behavior|1|Early retinal input can enter an auditory pathway
Normally, retinal axons project to visual targets such as the LGN, while ascending auditory input reaches the **medial geniculate nucleus**, or MGN. The MGN sends signals to primary auditory cortex, called A1. In the rewired-ferret preparation, this normally auditory thalamus-to-cortex connection remained available while its incoming input was experimentally altered.
Neonatal manipulations reduced the normal retinal targets and removed major auditory input to the MGN. Developing retinal axons then innervated the deprived auditory thalamus. The resulting route carried signals from the retina through the MGN to A1, allowing a cortical area normally associated with hearing to receive patterned visual input.
The intervention occurred while the pathways were developing, not after an adult cortex had been fully formed. Researchers later recorded visual responses in the altered pathway. The comparison therefore examined how early incoming activity helped shape a cortical circuit during development, rather than whether an adult ferret could learn to hear light through its ears.

## I06|R|roe|8|Rewired auditory neurons acquired visual selectivity
Roe and colleagues recorded individual neurons in auditory cortex after retinal input had been redirected through auditory thalamus. Moving light bars within particular parts of the visual field changed these cells' firing. The neurons therefore had **visual receptive fields**, despite being located in a cortical region that normally processed auditory input.
Some rewired A1 neurons preferred particular bar orientations or movement directions. Simple and complex receptive-field organizations were also present. These properties resembled features measured in normal visual cortex, indicating that organized visual selectivity could develop within cortical tissue receiving a different kind of early sensory input.
The responses were not identical to those in normal V1. Rewired A1 cells tended to have larger visual receptive fields, weaker visual responses, and longer electrically evoked input latencies. Early input changed important response properties while the altered pathway retained measurable differences in the precision and timing of its visual processing.

## I07|R|orientation|2|Visual orientation modules formed in auditory cortex
An **orientation module** contains neighboring cortical neurons with similar preferred edge angles. Sharma and colleagues measured the spatial organization of visual responses in rewired ferret A1. Regions preferring different orientations formed an organized map, extending the comparison beyond the response properties of one isolated neuron at a time.
Individual neurons within these regions had orientation tuning comparable to that of cells in normal visual cortex, but the overall map was less orderly. Long-range horizontal connections in rewired A1 also became more patchy and periodic than those in normal auditory cortex, although less so than the connections measured in V1.
Incoming visual activity therefore affected both local response selectivity and the organization of connections across cortex. The auditory cortical location did not prevent visual modules from developing. At the same time, the less orderly map and intermediate connection pattern preserved evidence that altered input had acted on tissue with its own developmental and anatomical constraints.

## I08|R|behavior|2|The rewired pathway could guide visual behavior
A visually responsive neuron does not by itself establish that an animal can use its activity to make a visual decision. Von Melchner, Pallas, and Sur tested behavior in ferrets with retinal projections redirected into the auditory pathway. They presented stimuli within a region of visual space served by that altered projection.
The animals responded to light in a way consistent with their learned visual response rather than their auditory response. The result distinguished visual behavior mediated through the changed pathway from the mere presence of an electrically active retinal connection. Input arriving through auditory thalamus could support a behavior that treated the event as visual.
The animals could also discriminate gratings differing in spatial frequency through the rewired route, although acuity was lower than through the normal visual pathway. Early rerouting therefore supplied usable visual information while preserving limits on the detail that could be resolved. Coarse pattern differences could guide a choice even when finer differences exceeded the resolving ability of the altered pathway.

## I09|R|PMC9633067|1|Restored sight requires visuomotor recalibration
A **cataract** is an opacity in the lens that reduces the pattern of light reaching the retina. Children born with dense cataracts in both eyes can receive patterned vision only after surgery. Senna and colleagues tested how these children and adolescents adjusted visually guided pointing after gaining access to clearer visual input.
Prism goggles shifted the apparent target position sideways. Normally sighted participants used feedback about their pointing errors to adjust subsequent reaches and showed an opposite error when the prisms were removed. Recently treated participants recalibrated less, and blurring vision in comparison participants did not reproduce the full reduction.
Recalibration improved with additional months and years of visual experience after surgery. Correcting the optical obstruction did not immediately supply a fully calibrated mapping from visual position to arm movement. The developing system continued learning from the relation between the seen target, the motor command, and the resulting hand position.

## I10|F|book|6.24|Removing sharp boundaries can improve recognition
A block-averaged picture can preserve an object's broad brightness pattern while adding conspicuous edges around each block. Those artificial boundaries introduce high spatial frequencies that were not part of the original object's structure. They can compete with the lower-frequency information carrying the broad arrangement needed to recognize the image.
In the original Lincoln demonstration, filtering out the high-frequency block edges makes the face easier to recognize even though fine detail has been removed. The filtered version preserves the broad light-dark arrangement while reducing an irrelevant set of strong boundaries. More visible edges do not always provide more useful information about identity.
Viewing from farther away produces a related change because the eye can no longer resolve the smallest boundaries. The two versions then look more alike. Spatial-frequency sensitivity therefore affects which image structures remain available at different viewing distances and which of those structures dominate the percept of an object.

## I11|R|PMC12586158|1|Visual neurons respond to contours that are absent
In a **Kanizsa illusion**, separated contour fragments can produce the appearance of an edge or surface spanning the empty region between them. The original study presented illusory bars and corresponding real bars to mice while recording visual-cortical neurons. Some V1 cells responded even though the region of their receptive field contained no printed bar.
The investigators changed the arrangement of the component fragments to distinguish responses to a completed bar from responses to one local segment. A specialized population responded to the global illusory arrangement rather than merely to each contributing piece. These were measured neural responses, not verbal reports of what the mice consciously perceived.
Targeted optical activation of the identified cells recreated a related population activity pattern without presenting the visual image. This **pattern completion** recruits a larger stored response from activation of part of the circuit. Feedback and recurrent cortical connections can therefore contribute information about an inferred contour beyond the contrast physically present at one retinal location.

## I12|F|book|6.30|Turning a face changes more than its visible parts
Face recognition depends partly on the arrangement of features, including the spacing of the eyes and their relation to the nose and mouth. Turning a face upside down preserves the individual parts but changes their familiar configuration relative to the observer. Detecting each part can remain easier than rapidly identifying the person from that unusual arrangement.
Newborn infants prefer some upright face-like patterns over corresponding inverted patterns. The original stimuli also changed contrast polarity, altering whether the eye and mouth regions had the light-dark arrangement typical of a naturally illuminated face. Orientation and contrast arrangement therefore affect viewing preferences before adult face-recognition skill has developed.
An early viewing preference and mature identity recognition are different abilities. A bias toward face-like patterns can increase exposure to faces, while later learning differentiates familiar individuals across expressions and viewpoints. The visual system must preserve stable relationships among features while tolerating the many changes that occur when a real person moves.

## I13|R|PMC11136492|1|Internal motion can bend an object's perceived path
The **double-drift illusion** combines two motions: a visible window moves through space while the texture inside it drifts in another direction. Observers can perceive the window traveling along a path that differs from its physical trajectory. Motion within the object changes the estimated position of the object containing it.
Steinberg and colleagues examined the illusion while observers tracked a moving fixation target. The target's movement kept the drifting stimulus relatively stable on the retina, yet the perceived trajectory could still deviate. The response therefore required considering information about eye movement together with the motion signals within the retinal image.
The illusion separates the physical path, retinal stimulation, and experienced path. These normally agree closely enough that their distinct contributions are difficult to notice. Here, combining the texture's drift with the window's motion shifted the position estimate over time, producing a systematic trajectory error rather than merely making the object difficult to see.

## I14|R|PMC11136492|3|Motion cortex carried the illusory trajectory signal
The double-drift study measured **BOLD fMRI**, a blood-oxygenation response related to local neural activity, while observers experienced different illusory paths. Several visual areas responded more strongly during the illusion than during a comparison condition. The researchers then asked whether patterns of activity contained information about which illusory trajectory was perceived.
Activity patterns in **hMT+**, the human motion-sensitive cortical complex, distinguished the illusory trajectories. Other measured visual regions did not provide the same successful readout. The result identified information within a regional population response rather than assuming that any increase in activity necessarily represented the object's perceived path.
Eye movements were recorded during scanning, and the trajectories could not be recovered from those eye-position measurements. The cortical result therefore was not explained by observers simply following different paths with their eyes. Retinal motion and information about gaze were combined within a representation carrying the systematically shifted trajectory.

## I15|F|book|6.14|Retinal motion and perceived motion can differ
An image can move across the retina because an object moved or because the eyes moved across a stationary scene. During a saccade across a page, the retinal image of the text shifts rapidly even though the words appear stationary. Interpreting motion therefore requires information about the cause of the image displacement.
A copy of an eye-movement command can inform sensory circuits that the retinal shift was self-generated. Signals from head movement and other senses contribute additional information about the observer's motion. Visual networks use these signals together with the pattern of image change to estimate which objects moved relative to the surrounding environment.
The converse also occurs during tracking: a pursued object can remain near the same retinal location while being perceived as moving through the world. Stable retinal position is therefore compatible with perceived motion when gaze changes appropriately. The estimated trajectory depends on combining retinal location over time with the changing direction of the eyes.
