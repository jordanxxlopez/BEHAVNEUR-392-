## 1|F|book|6.5|Vision begins with changes in retinal activity
**Sensation** begins when cells detect physical energy. In vision, that energy is light entering the eye. Photoreceptors in the retina, the thin neural tissue lining the back of the eye, change their electrical activity when they absorb light. Other retinal neurons transform those changes into signals sent toward the brain.
**Perception** is the experience of seeing an object, its color, or its movement. That experience requires the brain to combine signals from many retinal locations. A patch of light may belong to a prey animal, a person's face, or a reflection, depending on its arrangement and the surrounding scene.
Visual signals also guide actions before an observer describes what is present. A hunting fish turns toward a small moving target and adjusts its eyes before capturing it. The position and movement of that target must be converted into commands for the eyes, head, and body while the target continues to move.

## 2|F|book|6.2|Wavelength and intensity describe different inputs
**Wavelength** is the distance between successive peaks of a light wave. Human photoreceptors detect only part of the electromagnetic spectrum. Within that range, changing the mixture of wavelengths can change perceived hue. Ultraviolet light has shorter wavelengths than humans normally see, although some other animals have photopigments that detect it.
**Intensity** describes how much light reaches a surface or receptor. Increasing intensity usually makes a stimulus appear brighter, but perceived brightness also depends on its surroundings and the eye's adaptation. A dim object against a dark background can be easier to detect than a brighter object against an even brighter background.
**Saturation** describes how strongly a color differs from a neutral gray or white appearance. Mixing wavelengths can reduce saturation without simply making a light dimmer. Hue, brightness, and saturation describe perceptual dimensions, whereas wavelength and intensity describe physical inputs that the visual system must measure and compare.

## 3|R|PMC7383236|1|Ultraviolet light makes tiny prey stand out
Larval zebrafish eat tiny swimming organisms called **paramecia**. Yoshimatsu and colleagues filmed these organisms through different wavelength filters in a naturalistic tank. In the longer-wavelength view, background detail competed with the prey. In the ultraviolet view, illuminated paramecia appeared as bright points against a less cluttered background.
The researchers then presented real prey under ultraviolet or yellow illumination. Larvae produced prey-capture responses more readily under ultraviolet light. Those responses included inward eye rotation and tail movements. Removing ultraviolet-sensitive cones eliminated the usual advantage of ultraviolet illumination, identifying these photoreceptors as a source of the behaviorally useful signal.
The useful contrast depends on the animal's receptors and its environment. Human observers do not normally see this ultraviolet channel, so a scene that looks difficult to us can contain conspicuous prey signals for a fish. Underwater illumination, background reflections, and the prey's optical properties determine which receptor population receives the clearest signal.

## 4|F|book|6.4|The cornea and lens focus light on the retina
The **cornea**, the transparent front surface of the eye, bends incoming light. The lens provides additional focusing so that rays from an object meet at the retina. Because these optical structures redirect the rays, the retinal image is inverted vertically and reversed horizontally relative to the arrangement of objects outside the eye.
The **pupil** is the opening through the iris, the colored ring of muscle surrounding it. Changing pupil size regulates the amount of light entering the eye. The lens changes shape during **accommodation**, allowing near and distant objects to be brought into focus on the same retinal surface.
The six **extraocular muscles** rotate the eyeball to bring selected objects onto different retinal locations. Moving the eye changes which photoreceptors sample an object without moving the object itself. The brain must therefore combine the retinal signal with information about eye position to locate the object in the surrounding world.

## 5|R|PMC11521370|1|Living human cones can be imaged during seeing
The **foveola** is the small central region that supports the finest human spatial vision. Witten and colleagues used adaptive optics, a method that corrects optical distortions, to image individual cones there. Their instrument also delivered a tiny letter-shaped target while recording where that target fell on the living retinal mosaic.
Participants identified the orientation of an E-shaped target whose size changed across trials. The researchers measured both the spacing of the cones and the target's path across them. This made it possible to compare visual performance with the physical receptor arrangement that sampled the stimulus in each eye.
Even during attempted steady fixation, the target moved across neighboring cones because the eye made small movements. These movements changed the receptor signals available during the brief presentation. The experiment therefore measured spatial vision as an active sampling process, with receptor spacing and eye motion recorded together rather than inferred from a static photograph.

## 6|F|book|6.3|Rods and cones trade sensitivity for detail
**Rods** and **cones** are photoreceptors named for the shapes of their outer segments, the membrane-rich structures containing light-sensitive pigment. Rod pathways work effectively in dim conditions. Cone pathways support daylight color vision and fine detail. Their different sensitivity and wiring make the same scene appear different as illumination changes.
The central human fovea contains densely packed cones and no rods. Small retinal signals remain relatively separate there, preserving spatial detail. Away from the fovea, many receptors can contribute to a shared downstream neuron. Pooling helps detect weak light, while making the exact source within that pooled region harder to localize.
A faint star can become easier to detect when its image falls slightly away from the center of gaze. That places the light on rod-rich retina rather than the rod-free foveal center. Under these dim conditions, detecting the light and identifying its color or fine structure depend on different amounts and kinds of available information.

## 7|R|PMC7383236|2|An acute retinal zone improves prey detection
The larval zebrafish retina contains an **acute zone**, a region specialized for a behaviorally important part of visual space. Ultraviolet cones in this region view the upper frontal field where prey are captured. Their outer segments are unusually long, increasing the amount of pigment-containing membrane available to absorb incoming ultraviolet photons.
A tiny paramecium viewed from a distance can cover only one ultraviolet cone at a time. Increasing that cone's sensitivity therefore improves the chance that the prey produces a usable signal before it approaches closely. Regional specialization begins at the individual photoreceptor rather than waiting for many weak inputs to be combined in the brain.
During a hunting response, the eyes rotate inward and change where the prey falls on the two retinas. The same retinal specialization therefore samples different parts of external space as eye position changes. Receptor placement and eye movement work together to bring the target into the region with enhanced ultraviolet sensitivity.

## 8|F|book|6.8|Light reduces photoreceptor glutamate release
A **photopigment** contains an opsin protein bound to retinal, a light-sensitive molecule derived from vitamin A. Absorbing a photon changes retinal's shape and activates the opsin. In vertebrate rods and cones, the resulting chemical reactions reduce the concentration of cyclic GMP, a molecule that normally keeps certain membrane channels open.
As those channels close, inward positive current decreases and the photoreceptor **hyperpolarizes**, meaning its interior becomes more negative. This differs from a neuron becoming excited by opening an inward-current channel. The photoreceptor responds to increased light by reducing its electrical drive toward transmitter release at its synaptic ending.
In darkness, photoreceptors are relatively depolarized and release glutamate continuously. Light-induced hyperpolarization reduces calcium entry into the terminal and lowers glutamate release. Bipolar cells detect that decrease. Different bipolar-cell receptors then convert the same reduction in glutamate into either an increase or a decrease in their own electrical response.

## 9|R|PMC7383236|3|Synaptic calcium changes during a flash
Calcium entering a photoreceptor terminal helps trigger the release of glutamate-filled vesicles. Yoshimatsu and colleagues placed a fluorescent calcium indicator in ultraviolet-cone terminals in living zebrafish. The indicator changed brightness as calcium changed, allowing the researchers to follow individual cones during brief increases and decreases in ultraviolet illumination.
Bright flashes reduced terminal calcium, while dark flashes increased it. The response depended on retinal location. Cones in the prey-capture acute zone responded strongly to brief bright signals and recovered differently from cones elsewhere. A shared ultraviolet-sensitive opsin therefore operated within cells whose electrical and synaptic behavior had been regionally adjusted.
The fluorescent measurement follows calcium near the release machinery; it does not directly count released glutamate molecules. The researchers also measured glutamate with a separate sensor. Comparing the two signals allowed them to examine how an electrical response reaching the terminal was transformed into the chemical message received by neighboring retinal cells.

## 10|R|PMC12721711|3|Ribbon synapses support ongoing retinal signaling
Photoreceptors and bipolar cells release glutamate at specialized **ribbon synapses**. A ribbon is a protein structure that holds vesicles near the release site, helping organize transmitter delivery during continuing changes in membrane potential. The synapse can therefore convey both a sudden change and information that persists while illumination remains elevated.
In the mouse retina, transient and sustained ON ganglion cells receive input from different combinations of bipolar cells. The investigators reconstructed these connections with electron microscopy, which resolves the membranes and contact sites of individual cells. A visible ribbon at a bipolar terminal helped identify an excitatory contact onto a ganglion-cell dendrite.
The number and arrangement of vesicles near the ribbon can influence how long release continues after a stimulus begins. Differences in these vesicle pools were associated with the distinct output pathways. The study located an important transformation at the bipolar-cell terminal, where similar incoming voltage responses could produce differently timed glutamate signals.

## 11|F|book|6.8|ON and OFF pathways separate light changes
An **ON bipolar cell** becomes more active when light increases in its receptive-field center. In darkness, glutamate from the photoreceptor activates a receptor pathway that keeps a depolarizing channel closed. When light reduces glutamate release, that suppression is relieved and the bipolar cell depolarizes, increasing its output to downstream neurons.
An **OFF bipolar cell** uses a different glutamate receptor. Glutamate directly opens an excitatory channel, so reduced glutamate release during light makes the cell less active. A decrease in light increases photoreceptor release and excites the OFF pathway. The same photoreceptor signal can therefore be separated into complementary messages about brightening and darkening.
Ganglion cells convert these graded retinal signals into action potentials that travel along the optic nerve. An ON ganglion cell can signal a bright edge entering its center, while an OFF cell can signal a dark edge. Both pathways can use increased firing to announce their preferred kind of change.

## 12|R|PMC12721711|6|Glutamate release shapes response duration
A **transient** response is strongest near a change in illumination and then declines. A **sustained** response remains elevated while the stimulus continues. In the mouse retina, different ON ganglion cells display these two patterns even when they receive the same light stimulus, providing separate signals about change and continuing illumination.
The investigators measured light-driven bipolar-cell voltage and glutamate near ganglion-cell dendrites. Several presynaptic bipolar types had similar voltage responses, but the glutamate arriving at transient and sustained ganglion cells had different time courses. The difference therefore emerged during transmission through the bipolar output synapses rather than simply copying two different voltage inputs.
Repeated flashes also affected the pathways differently. Excitatory currents reaching transient ganglion cells were more strongly reduced by a closely following flash. This short-term change in synaptic transmission helps explain why one pathway emphasizes a new event while another continues carrying information about a stimulus that remains present.

## 13|R|PMC12585175|1|Retinal transporters alter wavelength-specific signals
An **excitatory amino acid transporter**, or EAAT, is a membrane protein that handles glutamate. Some retinal EAAT proteins also conduct chloride ions and participate in the electrical response to glutamate. In zebrafish ON pathways, these transporter-associated currents operate alongside metabotropic glutamate receptors at the first retinal synapse.
The researchers removed the genes for EAAT5b, EAAT7, or both and recorded an **electroretinogram**, an electrical response summed across retinal tissue. Removing both transporters reduced responses to ultraviolet-blue and red illumination while leaving the tested green-light response relatively preserved. The disruption therefore depended on which wavelength channel was stimulated.
The proteins also had different distributions across the retina, including the region specialized for prey capture. A moving ultraviolet target evoked altered hunting responses in transporter mutants. Transporter-associated chloride currents help transmit cone signals into ON bipolar cells, so disrupting these proteins changes the retinal message available for detecting a hunting target.

## 14|R|PMC7416113|1|Ganglion-cell activity can be followed in the live eye
**Retinal ganglion cells** receive processed signals from bipolar and amacrine cells and send their axons toward the brain. Zhou, Bear, and colleagues expressed a calcium indicator in zebrafish ganglion cells. Their microscope followed activity in both the cells' branching dendrites and the cell bodies during controlled visual stimulation.
The retina curves around the eye, so a straight scan would pass through different tissue layers. The researchers instead scanned along the curved ganglion-cell and dendritic layers. This kept the measured structures in view and allowed responses from different parts of the retina to be compared within a common anatomical arrangement.
Red, green, blue, and ultraviolet stimulus components changed independently. A ganglion cell could increase its calcium signal after one component brightened and after another darkened. Measuring these combinations revealed which spectral changes each output cell emphasized before its axon carried the processed message into the brain.

## 15|F|book|6.15|Center and surround emphasize local contrast
A neuron's **receptive field** is the region of visual space in which a stimulus changes its activity. Many retinal ganglion cells have a central region and an antagonistic surround. Light that excites an ON center can reduce the same cell's firing when it falls in the surrounding ring.
Horizontal cells combine signals across neighboring photoreceptors, while other retinal connections further shape center and surround responses. Because the two regions oppose one another, illuminating both can produce less change than illuminating the center alone. The output depends on a local brightness difference rather than simply the total number of photons reaching the eye.
An edge places different light levels across neighboring receptive fields. Cells whose centers lie on one side and surrounds extend across the boundary can respond strongly. Uniform illumination produces more cancellation between center and surround. This arrangement emphasizes boundaries that help separate objects from backgrounds and makes surrounding brightness influence the appearance of a patch.

## 16|R|PMC7416113|2|Retinal output separates onset from continuing light
Ganglion-cell dendrites occupy different depths within the **inner plexiform layer**, the retinal layer where bipolar cells contact ganglion cells. Some dendritic regions respond strongly when light begins, while others respond when it ends. Mapping activity by retinal position and layer depth reveals how these complementary pathways are arranged within the living eye.
The researchers presented a step of light followed by changing flicker. Different ganglion-cell populations emphasized the onset, offset, or rapidly varying part of the stimulus. A light remaining present and a sequence of light changes could therefore generate different output patterns even when they stimulated the same broad retinal region.
These response patterns were not distributed uniformly around the eye. Retinal regions viewing different parts of the world contained different mixtures of temporal and spectral signals. The optic nerve consequently carries multiple specialized descriptions of a scene, including where a change happened, which wavelengths changed, and how quickly the change developed.

## 17|F|book|6.9|Pooling changes sensitivity and spatial resolution
**Convergence** occurs when several neurons provide input to a shared downstream cell. In peripheral retina, many photoreceptors can contribute to one ganglion-cell output. A weak stimulus activating several receptors can then produce a combined response large enough to be transmitted, improving sensitivity when individual receptor signals are small.
The same pooling reduces spatial precision. If two nearby points activate receptors feeding the same output cell, that output alone cannot identify which receptor received each point. Separate signals from densely packed central cones preserve smaller differences in position and support the fine distinctions needed for reading or examining an object's surface.
Eye movements allow these different retinal regions to work together. Peripheral vision can detect an event that attracts attention, and a rapid gaze shift can bring it onto central retina for detailed inspection. The first detection and the later identification can therefore rely on different receptive-field sizes and different patterns of retinal convergence.

## 18|R|PMC12237402|2|Eye convergence moves prey into shared visual space
**Binocular overlap** is the part of the environment visible to both eyes. Mearns and colleagues compared hunting in several species of larval fish using high-speed recordings of eye position, tail shape, and prey location. In the cichlid species, the eyes rotated inward during hunting, expanding the shared view in front of the head.
The researchers tracked each eye relative to the animal's body axis rather than measuring only the animal's direction of travel. This separated eye convergence from a body turn toward the prey. During capture, cichlids tended to position prey centrally in the frontal region while coordinating eye and body movements.
Different species used different combinations of approach bouts and capture strikes. Their eye positions therefore had to be interpreted together with the prey's changing location. An inward eye rotation changes the two retinal images available for targeting, while the body movement changes the distance that the final strike must cover.

## 19|R|PMC11521370|4|Small retinal drifts improve visual sampling
During fixation, the human eye makes slow movements called **ocular drift** in addition to occasional rapid microsaccades. In the cone-resolved experiment, drift moved the tiny target across the receptor mosaic during its presentation. The researchers excluded trials with larger interruptions so they could examine the contribution of these small continuous movements.
The target's path tended to carry it toward retinal regions with denser cone sampling. Resolution was better than predicted from a model that treated the receptor array as completely stationary. Successive receptor measurements supplied changing information about the target's edges during the time available for the observer's orientation judgment.
The direction and extent of drift differed among eyes, and their contributions depended on the local cone arrangement. A target crossing a densely sampled region could generate more useful spatial comparisons than one remaining over a less favorable location. Fine human vision therefore depends partly on how the eye moves across its own receptor mosaic.

## 20|F|book|6.10|Each hemisphere receives the opposite visual field
The two optic nerves meet at the **optic chiasm**. Axons from the nasal half of each retina cross to the opposite side, while axons from the temporal half remain on the same side. Because the optics reverse the image, this partial crossing brings information from the same half of external space into one hemisphere.
The left visual field falls on the nasal retina of the left eye and the temporal retina of the right eye. Signals from both reach the right hemisphere. The right visual field reaches the left hemisphere through the complementary arrangement. Each hemisphere therefore receives information from both eyes rather than receiving an entire opposite eye.
Damage at different points produces different patterns of visual-field loss. An optic-nerve lesion removes input from one eye, while a lesion beyond the chiasm can remove corresponding portions of both eyes' visual fields. The arrangement of surviving vision can help identify which part of the pathway has been interrupted.

## 21|F|book|6.11|The LGN preserves eye and signal differences
The **lateral geniculate nucleus**, or LGN, is a thalamic relay between the retina and primary visual cortex. In primates, its main layers receive input from one eye at a time. Neighboring relay cells preserve an orderly relationship between locations in visual space, carrying a spatially organized signal toward the occipital cortex.
The **magnocellular** layers emphasize rapid changes and light-dark contrast, while **parvocellular** pathways preserve finer spatial detail and important cone comparisons. **Koniocellular** populations contribute additional signals, including short-wavelength cone information. These names describe distinct cell populations and routes, rather than three completely isolated versions of the visual world.
LGN neurons project to different layers and compartments of primary visual cortex. Cortical cells can then combine inputs that began with separate eyes or different retinal response properties. The pathway preserves useful distinctions during transmission while providing the connections needed for binocular and more complex visual responses to emerge.

## 22|R|PMC11981939|3|Visual responses can be matched to individual synapses
The **MICrONS** project combined recordings of mouse visual-cortex activity with a detailed reconstruction of the same tissue. The living animal viewed visual stimuli while calcium imaging measured responses from many neurons. Later, electron microscopy resolved the cells' branches and the synapses through which they contacted one another.
Matching the living and fixed-tissue images required recognizable anatomical landmarks and careful alignment. Once a recorded cell was identified in the reconstruction, its axon and dendrites could be traced through the volume. This linked a measured visual response to the physical input and output connections of that particular neuron.
The dataset includes primary visual cortex and neighboring higher visual areas. Researchers can therefore ask whether cells responding to similar visual features actually contact each other within an area or across area boundaries. The accompanying optical recording depicts visual-cortex imaging; the study's matched reconstruction supplies the evidence about individual synaptic contacts.

## 23|F|book|6.12|V1 maps visual position and combines inputs
**Primary visual cortex**, or V1, lies in the occipital lobe and receives a major projection from the LGN. Its **retinotopic map** preserves neighborhood relationships in visual space. Nearby cortical locations generally process nearby parts of the visual field, although the amount of cortical tissue devoted to each region is unequal.
Central vision occupies a disproportionately large cortical representation because it supplies densely sampled spatial information. A small region near fixation can therefore recruit more cortical tissue than an equally sized peripheral region. This **cortical magnification** reflects the high-resolution input provided by central retina and the processing devoted to it.
Inputs arriving in cortical layers are combined through local connections and passed to other layers and visual areas. Many cortical neurons respond to stimulation through either eye, unlike the eye-specific inputs in the principal primate LGN layers. Combining those signals makes comparisons between the two retinal images possible within the cortex.

## 24|R|PMC11981947|2|Similar visual tuning predicts cortical connections
Two neurons have similar **visual tuning** when the same kinds of visual changes tend to activate them. Using the MICrONS dataset, the investigators compared that functional similarity with actual reconstructed synapses. Neurons with more similar responses were more likely to be connected, both within visual areas and across area boundaries.
Nearby axons and dendrites have more opportunities to meet, so physical proximity had to be separated from selective contact formation. The analysis compared connected neurons with nearby alternatives whose branches also passed close together. Similar feature preferences still predicted finer details of synaptic connectivity beyond the opportunity created by neighboring branches.
A computational model separated which visual features activated a neuron from where its receptive field lay. Feature similarity predicted synaptic organization more strongly than receptive-field location alone. A visual circuit therefore contains selective connections among cells with related responses, rather than relying only on the fact that their branches occupy the same tissue.

## 25|F|book|6.36|Oriented edges activate selected cortical neurons
An **orientation-selective neuron** fires more strongly for an edge or bar at one angle than for the same feature at other angles. This can arise when several inputs with appropriately aligned receptive fields converge. An elongated light pattern can then activate the combined input more effectively than a small isolated spot.
A **simple cell** has spatially separated regions where light increases or decreases its firing. Both the edge's orientation and its precise position within the receptive field affect the response. A **complex cell** can preserve an orientation preference across a wider range of positions and often responds well as the preferred feature moves.
Different cells prefer different angles, so a contour activates a pattern across the population. The brain can use that population response to describe the edge's orientation while other neurons signal its location, color, and movement. Recognizing a complete object requires combining these local measurements across a much larger region of visual space.

## 26|R|PMC11521370|3|Cone spacing and eye drift act together
Cone density varies across the foveola and differs among human eyes. Closely packed receptors sample smaller spatial differences, but the retinal image does not remain fixed over them. Witten and colleagues measured the distance and direction of drift while participants judged tiny targets, relating these movements to the cone mosaic in each eye.
Drift length alone did not provide a complete account of performance. The target's starting position and the direction of its movement determined whether it approached a more densely sampled region. A movement of a given size could therefore change the available receptor information differently at different starting locations within the same small retinal area.
The recorded paths also varied across trials rather than following one identical trajectory. The researchers compared their distributions with the positions of dense cone regions and with measured resolution. These comparisons distinguish the physical receptor array from the changing sequence of receptor samples that the visual system receives during an actual visual judgment.

## 27|F|book|6.23|Spatial frequency describes coarse and fine structure
**Spatial frequency** describes how often light and dark alternate across visual space. Widely spaced stripes have low spatial frequency; tightly spaced stripes have high spatial frequency. The same object contains many spatial frequencies because broad light-dark regions, thin contours, and small surface details change brightness over different distances.
Visual frequency is often expressed in cycles per degree of visual angle. A cycle contains one complete light-dark repetition. Using visual angle describes the pattern reaching the eye, which changes with viewing distance. The same physical stripes occupy smaller retinal distances when the observer moves farther away from them.
Neurons respond preferentially to different ranges of spatial frequency. Low-frequency signals describe broad arrangements and large-scale shape, while high-frequency signals preserve fine edges and texture. An object's identity can depend on combining these ranges rather than treating any single spatial scale as a complete description of its appearance.

## 28|R|PMC12431771|1|Inferior temporal neurons retain spatial-scale signals
The **inferior temporal cortex**, or IT, is part of the primate ventral visual pathway involved in object recognition. Toosi and colleagues recorded individual macaque IT neurons while presenting faces and other images filtered into different spatial-frequency bands. The filtering changed which broad shapes or fine details remained available in each image.
The researchers also scrambled image structure to separate sensitivity to spatial scale from sensitivity to recognizable content. Neuronal responses still contained information about spatial frequency when the original objects were no longer recognizable. Spatial scale therefore remained measurable within a cortical region that also carries more complex information about visual categories.
Different cells preferred different filtered versions, and a population readout could distinguish the presented frequency bands. These responses preserve information about how an image is constructed across spatial scales. Recognizing an object does not require all early visual measurements to disappear as the signal reaches higher visual cortex.

## 29|R|PMC12431771|2|Coarse visual information emerges before fine detail
A neuron's response changes over the milliseconds following image onset. In the macaque IT recordings, information about low spatial frequencies could be recovered earlier than information about high spatial frequencies. The initial population response therefore emphasized coarse image structure before the later response placed greater weight on finer spatial details.
The average spatial-frequency preference shifted toward higher frequencies as the response developed. This shift was measured from changing firing patterns rather than by assuming that an object was recognized in one instantaneous step. A visual representation can evolve while the original image remains unchanged on the display.
Broad image structure can constrain possible object interpretations before fine features become available. Later detail can distinguish objects sharing the same general outline or arrangement. The temporal sequence recorded in IT supplies separate measurements of these stages, including an early preference for coarse structure and a later increase in fine-scale responses.

## 30|F|book|6.14|Ventral and dorsal pathways support different tasks
The **ventral visual stream** extends toward the temporal lobe and contributes to identifying objects, faces, and scenes. The **dorsal visual stream** extends toward posterior parietal cortex and contributes to spatial judgments and visually guided actions. Both receive processed visual information and exchange signals with other cortical regions.
Identifying a cup requires combining its contours, color, and familiar appearance. Picking it up also requires estimating its current position, orientation, and width relative to the hand. These are related computations, but successful recognition and successful control of a reach can be disrupted separately after different kinds of cortical damage.
A hunting animal faces the same distinction at another scale. Detecting a potential prey item must be followed by continuously updated estimates of where it lies and how to move toward it. Visual pathways can preserve information useful for identifying the target while other circuits transform its changing position into an appropriate movement.

## 31|R|PMC8547958|4|Pursuit keeps prey near a stable retinal region
Holmgren and colleagues measured head and eye movements as mice pursued crickets. They also reconstructed the surrounding environment so they could estimate what fell within each eye's view. This allowed the prey's trajectory to be expressed relative to the animal's changing gaze rather than only relative to the laboratory camera.
During pursuit, the mouse tended to hold the cricket within a restricted part of its visual field. That region also experienced relatively little background motion generated by the animal's own movement. Keeping the prey there reduced the amount of surrounding image displacement competing with the signal needed to track the target.
The eyes did not simply rotate independently to follow every movement of the cricket. Head orientation and compensatory eye movements worked together to establish the changing view. A prey item moving across the arena could therefore remain within a comparatively stable retinal region while the mouse itself changed position and approached.

## 32|F|book|6.16|Cone comparisons support human color vision
Human color vision normally begins with three cone classes whose pigments have overlapping spectral sensitivities. They are called **S**, **M**, and **L** cones for short-, medium-, and long-wavelength sensitivity. Each class responds across a range of wavelengths, so none functions as a detector for only one named color.
A single cone cannot determine whether its activation came from a favored wavelength at low intensity or a less effective wavelength at higher intensity. Comparing activity across different cone classes reduces this ambiguity. Two lights producing different relative S, M, and L responses can be distinguished even if they appear similarly bright.
Different mixtures of wavelengths can also produce the same three cone responses. Such mixtures can look identical despite having different physical spectra. Color perception therefore depends on the information available in receptor comparisons, followed by additional retinal and cortical processing, rather than on a direct readout of every wavelength present.

## 33|R|PMC8514090|1|Four cone classes send differently tuned outputs
Zebrafish have cone classes sensitive to ultraviolet, blue, green, and red portions of the spectrum. Yoshimatsu and colleagues measured calcium signals at the output terminals of each class while presenting lights of different wavelengths. Measuring the terminal response captured both the cone's own light sensitivity and influences arriving through retinal connections.
The output of a cone did not always follow the absorption spectrum of its opsin alone. Some wavelengths drove calcium changes in opposite directions within the same cone class. This **spectral opponency** means that retinal interactions had already converted wavelength differences into opposing signals at the first stage of synaptic output.
Red-cone outputs largely emphasized changes in overall light, while other cone outputs carried different wavelength comparisons. Ultraviolet cones also supplied signals useful for detecting bright prey. Multiple receptor classes therefore provided parallel outputs suited to different visual demands rather than four interchangeable copies of the same brightness measurement.

## 34|F|book|6.18|Opponent circuits compare cone signals
An **opponent channel** responds in opposite directions to different combinations of cone activity. In primates, important comparisons include L against M cone signals and S against a combination of L and M signals. These comparisons create signals related to color differences while other pathways emphasize changes shared across cone classes.
The comparison is implemented through excitatory and inhibitory connections among retinal cells. A ganglion cell can increase firing for one cone-weighted signal and decrease firing for the opposing signal. Its response therefore describes a difference between inputs, including differences across the center and surround of its receptive field.
Trichromatic and opponent accounts describe different stages of the same system. Three cone classes provide the initial measurements, and retinal circuits recombine them into comparison signals. A color's appearance then depends on those signals, adaptation, and cortical context rather than on three cones independently announcing red, green, and blue percepts.

## 35|R|PMC11377037|2|Mouse cortex compares ultraviolet and green contrast
Mouse cones provide strong ultraviolet and green-sensitive signals, with their contribution varying across the retina. Color-opponent responses were especially common in posterior V1, which represents the upper visual field. This spatial bias corresponded to differences in the visual information encountered above and below the animal. ## 36|R|PMC7416113|3|Color and timing are combined in retinal output

The output reaching the brain contains these combined descriptions. A short ultraviolet-bright event and a prolonged blue-dark event can recruit different ganglion-cell populations even when they occur at the same retinal location. Wavelength and response duration are already represented together before higher visual areas combine the signal with movement and behavioral context.

## 38|F|book|6.20|Adaptation changes the color balance after viewing
A **negative afterimage** appears in colors complementary to those of a recently viewed image. Fixating a colored region for several seconds changes the responses of the receptors and downstream opponent pathways sampling it.
