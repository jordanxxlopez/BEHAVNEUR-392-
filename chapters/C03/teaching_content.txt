## 1 F B1 The nervous system carries signals between brain and body
The **central nervous system**, or CNS, consists of the brain and spinal cord. The **peripheral nervous system**, or PNS, includes nerves and ganglia outside those structures. Together, these divisions carry information from sensory receptors, process that information, and send commands to muscles and glands throughout the body.
A **nerve** is a bundle of axons in the peripheral nervous system. A **ganglion** is a collection of neuronal cell bodies outside the brain and spinal cord. These terms identify different kinds of tissue: the bundle carries signals, while the ganglion contains cells that send or receive them.
An animal catching prey must detect the target, turn toward it, and coordinate many muscles. Sensory signals enter central circuits through peripheral pathways, and motor commands return to the body. In the recording, larval zebrafish track prey through coordinated turns and swimming, making cooperation between sensory input and motor output directly visible.
## 2 F B2 Anatomical directions depend on the nervous system's axis
The **neuraxis** is an imaginary line running through the brain and spinal cord. **Rostral** means toward the nose, and **caudal** means toward the tail or lower spinal cord. These directions describe the position of one structure relative to another along the nervous system's main axis.
**Dorsal** means toward the back, while **ventral** means toward the belly. The human neuraxis bends between the forebrain and spinal cord. Dorsal therefore points toward the top of the forebrain but toward the back of the spinal cord, rather than toward one fixed direction in the room.
**Medial** means toward the midline, and **lateral** means toward the side. **Ipsilateral** structures lie on the same side; **contralateral** structures lie on opposite sides. A left-sided brain region can send signals to a right-sided muscle, so the side of the source and the side of its target both matter.

## 4 F B2 Brain sections expose different views of the same anatomy
A **coronal section** cuts across the brain from side to side, separating front from back. A **sagittal section** separates left from right. A **horizontal section** separates upper from lower portions. Each plane reveals a different arrangement of the same structures rather than a different kind of brain.
A section through the midline is **midsagittal**. It exposes structures that may be hidden when the brain is viewed from its outer surface, including the corpus callosum and parts of the ventricular system. A section farther to the side passes through a different set of structures within one hemisphere.
Microscopes can also collect images at several depths through intact tissue. The accompanying recording shows spontaneous activity in a larval brain, viewed through an optical imaging method. Identifying the viewing direction and the tissue depth is necessary before comparing a fluorescent region with a structure in a physical section.

## 6 F B8 Gray and white matter organize cell bodies and axons
**Gray matter** contains many neuronal cell bodies, dendrites, and synapses. **White matter** contains many myelinated axons traveling between locations. The color difference reflects tissue composition. Both contain supporting cells and blood vessels, and both participate in communication by placing different parts of the signaling pathway in different locations.
The **cerebral cortex** is a sheet of gray matter covering the cerebral hemispheres. Much of the underlying white matter carries axons between cortical regions and deeper structures. A cross section reveals this arrangement more clearly than a view of the outer surface, where the internal bundles remain hidden.
Fluorescent brain recordings emphasize labeled cells and their activity rather than the natural color of gray and white matter. The accompanying spontaneous-activity recording uses a cellular indicator in larval zebrafish. Interpreting it requires distinguishing the anatomical tissue being imaged from the fluorescence signal used to observe that tissue.

## 8 F B3 Three meningeal layers surround brain and spinal cord
The **meninges** are protective membranes surrounding the brain and spinal cord. The outer **dura mater** is tough connective tissue. Beneath it lies the **arachnoid membrane**, and the delicate **pia mater** follows the surface of the nervous tissue, including the contours of its folds and grooves.
The **subarachnoid space** lies between the arachnoid and pia. It contains cerebrospinal fluid and major blood vessels that travel along the brain's surface. This fluid-filled compartment surrounds nervous tissue externally, while cavities within the brain contain another continuous portion of the cerebrospinal fluid system.
These layers define compartments that matter for injury and clinical treatment. Bleeding into one space can press on nearby tissue, and infection can inflame the membranes. An injection or surgical approach must pass through specific layers, so the location of the dura, arachnoid, and pia determines which tissues it reaches.
## 9 F B4 Ventricles contain cerebrospinal fluid inside the brain
The **ventricles** are fluid-filled cavities within the brain. The two **lateral ventricles** lie within the cerebral hemispheres and communicate with the **third ventricle** near the midline. A narrow passage called the **cerebral aqueduct** carries fluid from the third ventricle toward the **fourth ventricle** beside the hindbrain.
The **choroid plexus** produces much of the cerebrospinal fluid, or **CSF**, within these cavities. Fluid passes through openings into spaces around the brain and spinal cord. Its movement involves several routes and local forces, rather than a single rigid pipe that carries all fluid at one constant speed.
The spinal cord also contains a **central canal**, continuous with the ventricular system. In larval zebrafish, a thin structure called the **Reissner fiber** lies within this canal. In the recording, the fiber moves inside a living animal, providing a direct view of a small structure within a fluid-filled central compartment.

## 11 F B4 Cilia move fluid along specialized internal surfaces
A **cilium** is a thin projection extending from a cell's surface. Some cilia beat repeatedly and exert force on surrounding fluid. Cells lining parts of the ventricular and central-canal system carry motile cilia, placing moving cellular structures directly at the boundary between nervous tissue and cerebrospinal fluid.
Ciliary movement can create local fluid motion even when the surrounding tissue remains in place. The direction and coordination of beating affect how nearby particles move. Local movement near a cilium and net transport along a larger compartment describe different spatial scales of the same fluid environment.

This scene makes the canal's lining recognizable as an active cellular surface, rather than an empty wall around a passive reservoir of fluid.

## 17 F B6 Developmental divisions organize the major brain regions
The **forebrain**, **midbrain**, and **hindbrain** are major divisions established during development. In the adult brain, each division contains several recognizable structures with different cell populations and projections. Developmental names describe the origin of these regions, while their mature anatomy identifies the pathways and nuclei that operate within them.
The forebrain includes the **telencephalon** and **diencephalon**. The telencephalon contains the cerebral hemispheres, including cortex, basal ganglia, and several limbic structures. The diencephalon includes the thalamus and hypothalamus, which occupy deeper positions near the third ventricle and communicate extensively with other central regions.
The midbrain forms the **mesencephalon**. The hindbrain includes the **metencephalon**, which gives rise to the pons and cerebellum, and the **myelencephalon**, which gives rise to the medulla. These divisions organize a continuous nervous system, with pathways crossing developmental boundaries as they travel between the forebrain, brainstem, and spinal cord.
## 18 F B9 Cortical folds separate lobes without isolating their circuits
A **gyrus** is a ridge on the cerebral surface, and a **sulcus** is a groove between ridges. Deep grooves help identify the **frontal**, **parietal**, **temporal**, and **occipital lobes**. These visible landmarks provide a shared map for locating cortical areas across different views of the brain.
The frontal lobe contains primary motor cortex and regions involved in organizing behavior. Parietal regions process bodily and spatial information. Temporal regions participate in hearing, object recognition, and memory, while occipital regions contain major visual-processing areas. Each lobe contains several areas rather than serving one single psychological function.
Axons travel within and between these lobes, so a behavior usually requires communication across several regions. Recognizing an object and reaching toward it recruits visual processing, spatial information, movement planning, and motor output. The boundaries named on the surface map help locate these operations without separating them into independent organs.
## 19 F B10 Sensory and motor areas occupy distinct cortical locations
The **primary visual cortex** lies in the occipital lobe, and the **primary auditory cortex** lies in the temporal lobe. **Primary somatosensory cortex** receives information about the body and lies just behind the central sulcus. These areas receive organized inputs that preserve important features of the sensory information.
**Primary motor cortex** lies just in front of the central sulcus. Its neurons contribute descending signals that influence movement through brainstem and spinal pathways. Neighboring cortical locations often influence neighboring body regions, but the map contains overlapping populations and does not function as a row of isolated muscle switches.
**Association cortex** processes information beyond the earliest cortical sensory stages or combines information needed for behavior. A visual target must be recognized and located before a coordinated reach can occur. Axons between cortical areas and subcortical structures allow incoming sensory information to guide movement rather than remaining a separate sensory experience.

## 22 F B10 Descending pathways carry cortical signals toward muscles
A **descending pathway** carries signals from higher central regions toward lower brainstem or spinal targets. Neurons in motor cortex send axons into several such pathways. Some influence spinal circuits relatively directly, while others act through brainstem structures that organize posture, coordination, and the timing of movement.
Many motor pathways cross the midline, producing strong **contralateral** control of the body. Crossing does not mean that each hemisphere operates alone or influences only one side. Bilateral projections, brainstem pathways, and spinal interneurons allow signals from one hemisphere to affect coordinated movements involving both sides of the body.
The final command to a skeletal muscle leaves through a **motor neuron**, whose axon reaches muscle fibers outside the central nervous system. Cortical activity must therefore act through intervening circuits and motor neurons. The pathway from a cortical cell to a moving limb contains several anatomically distinct stages.

## 25 F B11 Commissural axons carry signals between hemispheres

It contains axons traveling between cortical regions in the left and right cerebral hemispheres beneath the cortical surface.
These axons allow information processed on one side to influence activity on the other. Coordinating the hands, combining sensory information, and organizing behavior across both sides of space all require interhemispheric communication. ## 26 F B12 The hippocampus contributes information about context
The **hippocampus** is a curved structure within the medial temporal region. It communicates with surrounding cortex and several subcortical targets. Its neurons help represent places, events, and their context, allowing an animal to distinguish where something happened and use that information when choosing what to do later.
A **context** includes the surrounding features that make one situation different from another. The same sound or odor can have different consequences in different locations. Remembering a dangerous place therefore requires more than detecting the cue itself; it requires combining the cue with information about the environment in which it occurred.
The hippocampus does not act as a storage box separated from the rest of the brain. Its outputs influence cortical and subcortical circuits that guide action. One route reaches the hypothalamus, providing a way for information about remembered surroundings to alter defensive responses such as avoiding a location or running toward shelter.

## 34 F B13 Basal ganglia form deep forebrain circuits for action

## 36 F B13 The thalamus routes information through specialized nuclei
The **thalamus** lies deep in the forebrain beside the third ventricle. It contains many **nuclei**, or collections of neuronal cell bodies within the central nervous system. Different nuclei receive different inputs and project to different cortical targets, creating several organized routes through the same major structure.
Visual information passes through the **lateral geniculate nucleus** toward visual cortex, while auditory information passes through the **medial geniculate nucleus** toward auditory cortex. Other nuclei carry somatosensory information or participate in motor and cognitive circuits. Most sensory systems therefore involve thalamic processing before their major cortical stages.
The thalamus also receives extensive feedback from cortex and other subcortical structures. Its neurons can alter the timing and conditions under which information reaches cortical populations. This reciprocal organization makes it an active component of distributed circuits, rather than a collection of wires that always transmits every input without modification.

## 38 F B14 The hypothalamus coordinates behavior and internal state
The **hypothalamus** lies below the thalamus around the lower portion of the third ventricle. Its nuclei regulate feeding, drinking, temperature, reproduction, and responses to danger. These functions require coordination between behavior and the body's internal conditions, rather than control of a single sensory modality or one group of muscles.
Hypothalamic outputs influence the **autonomic nervous system**, which regulates organs, and the **endocrine system**, which releases hormones into the blood. Other projections reach brainstem circuits organizing movement and defensive responses. Different outputs allow a change in environmental conditions to recruit bodily adjustments and overt actions together.
The anterior hypothalamic nucleus participates in defensive behavior, but neighboring hypothalamic populations have different targets and functions. Activating a selected population can alter locomotion or posture through descending pathways. The direction of its axons determines which downstream circuits receive that signal and which components of the bodily response it can influence.
