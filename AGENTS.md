## Teaching prose and transcript requirements: all chapters

Follow [course/TEACHING_STYLE.md](course/TEACHING_STYLE.md) for every chapter, every slide paragraph, and every speaker-note teaching transcript, including authorized revisions. The instructor rejected generic research-summary prose in Chapter 4 and supplied a preferred plain-language rewrite. Match that example's connected explanatory sentences, definitions woven into the prose, and clear descriptions of what changes in the cell. Teach the actual biology: identify the relevant molecule or structure, explain what it does, specify what changes, and describe the resulting cellular or behavioral response. Define unfamiliar machinery in ordinary language as it is introduced. Explain the action behind phrases such as "recruits intracellular signaling" or "changes cellular function" without replacing them with a dense chain of unexplained protein names.

Every paragraph, including paragraph three, adds substantive information from the sources. Do not close with a statement about what the study brings together, places in a broader context, illustrates, highlights, connects, or teaches. Do not replace explanation with "access, target location, and cellular machinery all matter" or another obvious list of factors. State the specific transport process, receptor location, signaling step, time-dependent change, inhibitor result, anatomy, or behavioral finding instead. A correct but empty summary is still unacceptable.

Read each slide and transcript aloud as a professor explaining the material. Sentences must develop one another, not stack unexplained technical names or repeat the title. Apply the deletion test: if a sentence can disappear without losing a definition, mechanism, source-specific finding, experimental detail, or necessary scientific distinction, remove or replace it. A plain-language conclusion is welcome when it explains the biological change, as in the instructor's supplied example. A regex pass is not a teaching-quality pass. Review the third paragraph separately for generic wrap-up language. Keep this course's 5–7 rendered lines per paragraph; never import Neuroethology's older sentence quota or pad a paragraph to satisfy length. For the current Chapter 4 revision, replace only the three content paragraphs and matching teaching transcripts. Preserve all titles, slide order, layout, figures, recordings, captions, credits, and takeaways.

## Teaching and balance

Use 30–40% textbook foundations and 60–70% primary-research teaching, adjusted to the chapter. At the preferred count, 29 foundation slides and 44 research slides give approximately 40%/60%. Foundation slides teach mechanisms from the textbook even when an existing study figure illustrates the concept; retagging alone is insufficient. Tag each content slide instructional_role as foundation or research; do not count an incidental citation as research teaching. The textbook defines chapter scope and concepts. Interweave concept teaching with directly related primary studies rather than separating a foundation block from a research block. Connect studies to the topic and mechanism just introduced; emphasize current research while revisiting essential older work. Never say "the textbook" or add textbook metacommentary in visible teaching prose. References and source-image credits still identify the textbook accurately. Review every video and added image for its relationship to the adjacent concept, not merely its availability. Teach each concept, then use source-supported study findings to explain how it works. Use a manageable group of core papers across several slides rather than a different paper on every slide. Include current accessible research and foundational work where needed; verify references and quantitative claims.

Every paragraph teaches material. Use direct, concrete teaching sentences in three paragraphs. Each content paragraph must occupy 5–7 full rendered lines, checked against the exported PDF. Expand explanations rather than add filler, forced line breaks, or blank-line padding. The line requirement supersedes the earlier 70–110-word target. Identify the actor, action, and result. Do not summarize with vague verbs such as connected, linked, supported, or relationship. For example: "The neurons move the paddle. A hit or miss determines the electrical signal sent back to them." Scientific connections can still be described literally when their anatomy is the subject. Use three substantive paragraphs per content slide and natural complete-sentence teaching transcripts in notes, adapting depth to this easier class and readable layout. Do not pad paragraphs to meet a sentence count. No agendas, learning objectives, previews, fixed method-result-limitation templates, presentation commentary, or em dashes in visible text. Do not end paragraphs on routine limitations or stock caution. The instructor will discuss limitations and the importance of controls orally. Preserve scientifically necessary distinctions and genuine experimental conditions; do not turn hypotheses into facts. No p-values, error bars quoted in prose, test names, or sample-size bookkeeping. Retain quantities that explain the concept. Finish with 5–6 exam-level takeaways with bold lead phrases.
